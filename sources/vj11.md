## Abstract
Vertical axis wind turbines (VAWTs) have regained attention for distributed, urban, and floating offshore applications, yet the literature remains fragmented across competing rotor concepts and modelling traditions. This review consolidates the principal archetypes-Savonius, H-Darrieus, troposkein Darrieus, helical Darrieus, and Savonius-Darrieus hybrids-through five governing parameters: drag-versus-lift-driven operating principle, tip speed ratio ? = ?R/V? (0.6-1.2 for Savonius; 2.5-5.0 for Darrieus), solidity ? = Nc/R (0.1-0.4), chord-based Reynolds number Re_c (10^5-10^6), and peak power coefficient Cp_max (0.15-0.25 for Savonius; 0.35-0.45 for optimized H-Darrieus). Off-design performance is dominated by unsteady mechanisms that quasi-steady streamtube models cannot resolve-leading edge vortex shedding, dynamic stall hysteresis, blade-wake interaction, and flow-curvature-induced virtual camber-each examined for its contribution to the instantaneous torque CT(?) and the cycle-averaged Cp. Turbulence closures are benchmarked against phase-locked PIV and torque measurements: k-?SST URANS captures peak-region Cp to within ?5-10% but over-predicts torque below ?opt; the ?-Re_? transition SST model reduces this error to ?3-5%; DES, DDES, and LES reach ?2-3% at one to two orders of magnitude higher cost. Best practice computational fluid dynamics (CFD) guidelines are consolidated: domain extents of 15D upstream, 10D downstream, and 20D lateral; rotating sub-domain Drot ? 1.5D; y+ ? 1; ?? ? 0.1?; and 20-30 revolutions before sampling. Performance enhancement strategies (variable pitch, guide vanes, helical twist, and hybridization) are reviewed quantitatively, with reported Cp gains of 5-30%. Four research priorities are identified: (i) transition-sensitive turbulence closures validated below Re_c = 5 ? 10^5; (ii) coupled aero-hydro-servo-elastic models for floating offshore VAWTs; (iii) machine-learning-augmented turbulence modelling-including physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and neural-network-corrected RANS closures-to improve unsteady flow prediction at sub-LES cost; and (iv) integrated aeroacoustic-aeroelastic frameworks for urban and building-integrated deployment.

### 1. Introduction

The global transition towards decarbonized energy systems has accelerated investment in wind power technology at an unprecedented rate. By 2023, cumulative global wind capacity exceeded 900 GW, with projections indicating a tripling of installed capacity by 2050 to meet net-zero targets [1]. Conventional large-scale horizontal axis wind turbines (HAWTs) dominate utility scale installations; however, their deployment is constrained by land use regulations, noise ordinances, visual impact, and—critically—their requirement for unidirectional, low turbulence inflow conditions. In contrast, the rapid densification of urban environments and the proliferation of distributed energy generation concepts have revived substantial academic and industrial interest in VAWTs [1,2]. VAWTs exhibit four properties that are aerodynamically and structurally advantageous in non-ideal inflows: (i) omnidirectionality (is intrinsic to all VAWT families because the rotor axis is perpendicular to the horizontal wind component, so the swept rectangle D × H presents an identical projection regardless of compass direction), eliminating the yaw mechanism and the associated control hardware; (ii) a low centre of gravity, simplifying support structures and floating offshore platforms; (iii) low tip speed, reducing aerodynamic noise generation and allowing safer integration in inhabited environments; and (iv) tolerance of high turbulence intensity (TI) and skewed inflow (yaw angles up to ±30◦) [3–6]. These properties motivate three application classes: building-integrated and rooftop micro-generation; closely spaced urban or suburban arrays; and floating offshore platforms in deep water sites. VAWTs harvest wind energy from any azimuthal direction without the need for active yaw control systems. This property, combined with lower structural gravity centres, reduced noise signatures, and compatibility with low-height rooftop and near-building installations, positions VAWTs as primary candidates for smallscale and micro wind applications in the built environment [2,4,7]. The built environment is characterized by highly turbulent, rapidly varying wind conditions with significant directional shifting, conditions under which HAWTs suffer marked efficiency degradation whilst certain VAWT configurations can maintain operational stability [5]. The two principal VAWT families—Savonius and Darrieus—embody fundamentally different aerodynamic operating principles. The Savonius rotor, conceived by the Finnish engineer Sigurd J. Savonius in 1922, operates primarily on aerodynamic drag differential between its advancing and returning bucket shaped blades, resulting in low TSR ≈0.7–1.5 and modest Cp ≤0.25 [8,9]. The Darrieus turbine, patented by Georges Jean Marie Darrieus in 1931, generates lift force through symmetric airfoil blades, achieving substantially higher Cp values (up to 0.42–0.45 in optimized designs) at elevated TSR [10–12]. Hybrid configurations coupling both rotors attempt to reconcile the superior self-starting capability of the Savonius with the aerodynamic efficiency of the Darrieus, whilst helical blade geometries address the torque ripple inherent to straight-blade configurations [10]. Despite a research legacy spanning nine decades, fundamental aerodynamic challenges persist. The flow field surrounding a rotating VAWT blade is intrinsically unsteady and dominated by dynamic stall, LEV shedding, BWI, and flow-curvature-induced virtual camber [13–16]. These mechanisms resist closed form analytical treatment and remain numerically demanding to capture, particularly at the moderate to low Re_c < 5 × 105 typical of micro VAWTs. Prior review articles have addressed VAWTs from individual perspectives: Tjiu et al. [11] focused on Darrieus configuration taxonomy; Akwa et al. [1] on Savonius performance; Bhutta et al. [17] on geometric variants; Tummala et al. [18] on small-scale turbines; and Zhao et al. [19] on lift type aerodynamic enhancement. None of these prior works integrates aerodynamic theory, unsteady flow physics, CFD methodology, experimental validation, design optimization, and emerging applications (floating offshore,

ML-augmented modelling, aeroacoustic–aeroelastic coupling) into a single critical synthesis with quantitative comparative tables. The review is structured as follows: Section 2 classifies VAWT configurations and describes their geometry. Section 3 develops the aerodynamic fundamentals and governing parameters. Section 4 provides a critical performance comparison. Section 5 evaluates numerical modelling approaches. Section 6 surveys experimental investigations. Section 7 reviews design optimization and performance enhancement strategies. Section 8 presents conclusions and future research directions.

### 2. VAWT Classification and Geometrical Configurations

VAWTs can be broadly divided into three aerodynamic categories: drag-driven, liftdriven, and hybrid machines [12,20].

#### 2.1. Drag-Driven VAWTs

The Savonius rotor, the archetype of drag-driven VAWTs (invented by S. J. Savonius in 1929), extracts energy primarily through the differential drag on its concave and convex surfaces. It offers high starting torque and simple construction, but is limited to a maximum power coefficient of approximately 0.25, which confines its use mainly to small-scale pumping or low power applications [12]. Savonius Rotor The Savonius rotor is the simplest and most widely deployed VAWT configuration, consisting of two or more semi-cylindrical or semi-elliptical blades mounted on a central shaft with a defined overlap ratio between the concave faces [1]. The overlap ratio (e/d, where e is the gap between blade edges and d is the blade diameter) fundamentally governs both the drag asymmetry that drives rotation and the internal flow that partially recovers energy from the returning blade. Optimized overlap ratios typically fall in the range of 0.10–0.20 [2,4]. The aspect ratio (H/D, height-to-diameter) influences three-dimensional end effects and structural rigidity; aspect ratios between 1.0 and 2.0 are generally adopted for power generation applications, with higher values favouring reduced tip losses at the cost of increased structural loads [7]. The number of blades (two, three, or modified S profiles) alters the flow interference pattern; two-bladed designs are the most common, offering a balance between self-starting torque and aerodynamic efficiency [2]. The Savonius operates at low TSR values (0.7–1.5) and achieves maximum power coefficients of 0.15–0.25 in optimized configurations under controlled laboratory conditions [1], though typical field deployed units perform considerably below these values due to turbulent inflow and Reynolds number effects. The semi-cylindrical Savonius rotor is among the simplest VAWT configurations to manufacture, with a blade geometry that avoids expensive fabrication processes such as blade moulding or 3D printing [21]. Helical (twisted) Savonius configurations distribute the torque more evenly over the blade span, producing a more uniform time variation in the torque coefficient compared with conventional straight-blade rotors; however, this improvement is accompanied by a modest reduction in the maximum power coefficient—from approximately 0.12 to 0.11 (∼8%) for a rotor with a 45◦twist angle [22]. Savonius rotors are inherently self-starting owing to a positive static torque coefficient at every azimuthal position, which is the principal reason why they serve as primer stages in hybrid H-Savonius configurations [23]. Within one revolution, a two-bladed straight Savonius produces two torque peaks from the advancing and returning blade cycles, with the negative torque generated on the returning blade constituting a well-known limitation of the basic design [24].

Recent investigations have explored non-conventional blade profiles—including Bachtype, Benesh, and twisted (helical) Savonius geometries—which redistribute aerodynamic torque more uniformly over the rotation cycle [8,25]. Twisted Savonius designs, in particular, suppress torque pulsation (reducing the torque ripple coefficient by up to 30%) whilst marginally improving Cp [1]. Guide vanes and shielding plates have also been studied as passive devices to reduce the drag on the returning blade, yielding Cp improvements of 5–15% in some configurations [2].

#### 2.2. Lift-Driven VAWTs

This category encompasses the Darrieus-type family, introduced by G. J. M. Darrieus in 1931, which relies on aerodynamic lift generated by rotating airfoil blades. This category includes the original curved blade (“egg-beater”) rotor, the straight-bladed H-rotor (often called giromill), and helical blade variants [11,12]. Owing to their inherently higher aerodynamic efficiency—with power coefficients exceeding 0.4—lift-driven machines are the dominant configuration for utility scale and building mounted electricity generation.

#### 2.2.1. Curved-Blade Darrieus (Troposkein)

The original Darrieus design employs curved blades whose shape approximates the troposkein—the equilibrium curve adopted by a flexible rope rotating at constant angular velocity about its axis—thereby minimizing bending stresses under centrifugal loading [9,10]. This geometry results in a characteristic “egg-beater” appearance. The aerodynamic chords remain tangential to the troposkein curve, producing variable effective angle of attack as a function of blade azimuthal position and contributing to a more gradual power-producing region compared to H-rotors [11]. Troposkein turbines were extensively developed in the 1970s–1990s—most notably by Sandia National Laboratories (USA) and the National Research Council of Canada—and remain the basis for several large commercial designs up to 500 kW [10].

#### 2.2.2. H-Rotor (Straight-Blade Darrieus)

The H-rotor, also termed giromill or straight-blade Darrieus, employs blades of constant chord length and symmetric (or occasionally cambered) airfoil cross section arranged parallel to the rotation axis and connected to the hub by horizontal struts [12,26]. This geometry offers several manufacturing and aerodynamic advantages: uniform chord Reynolds number distribution along the blade span (ignoring tip effects), simpler blade fabrication, and easier integration of variable pitch mechanisms [9,27]. For lift-driven Darrieus type vertical axis wind turbines, solidity (σ = Nc/R where N is blade number, c is chord, and R is rotor radius) is the primary geometric parameter controlling the tip speed ratio at peak power coefficient. Lower solidity shifts the optimal λ  λopt  to higher values: the optimum tip speed ratio decreases inversely with solidity, and the smaller the solidity, the higher the optimal tip speed ratio and the wider the range of tip speed ratios over which the turbine maintains a high power coefficient [28,29]. Systematic URANS studies have further demonstrated that λopt is invariant to the newly introduced parameter σλ3, regardless of the turbine geometrical and operational characteristics [29]. The underlying kinematic constraint is that the relative-velocity-induced angle of attack envelope must remain bounded by the static stall angle for efficient attached flow operation; a rotor of lower solidity must rotate faster to maintain the same range of effective angles of attack. This relationship is characteristic of lift-driven machines and does not extend to drag-driven Savonius rotors. Drag-type VAWTs such as the Savonius rotor typically operate at lower tip speed ratios (peak power coefficient occurring at λ ≈0.8–1.0) than lift based VAWTs such as Darrieus rotors [24,28], and their performance is determined primarily by differential drag, bucket geometry, and overlap ratio, not by the same solidity—

λ inverse proportionality. NACA four-digit symmetric profiles (NACA 0012, 0015, 0018, 0021) have been extensively studied as H-rotor blade sections; the choice of thickness-tochord ratio critically influences dynamic stall onset TSR, maximum Cp, and self-starting capability [12,30]. The strut drag penalty is a significant concern for H-rotors: parasitic drag from horizontal connecting arms typically reduces net Cp by 5–12% relative to blade-only simulations [31,32]. Blade–strut junction geometry optimization and the use of streamlined strut profiles have been identified as effective mitigation strategies [33]. The number of blades (typically two to five) governs the torque ripple frequency and amplitude; threebladed configurations represent the most common compromise between self-starting torque uniformity and reduced blade–wake interaction [34].

#### 2.2.3. Helical Darrieus

Helical or twisted H-rotors wrap the blade in a helical pattern around the rotation axis through a prescribed twist angle (typically 60–120◦). This geometric modification staggers the blade azimuthal positions continuously, distributing the torque ripple over the full rotation cycle and effectively eliminating the near-zero torque troughs that plague straight-blade designs [11,35]. The structural complexity and manufacturing cost of helical blades are substantially higher than H-rotors, limiting their commercial adoption to highend small-scale applications [36]. Aerodynamically, helical designs exhibit moderate improvements in Cp consistency and noise reduction, particularly in turbulent inflow conditions [18].

#### 2.3. Hybrid VAWTs

A drag-driven Savonius starter is combined with a lift-driven Darrieus section to overcome the poor self-starting capability of many Darrieus designs while retaining high peak efficiency; such configurations are frequently studied for urban installations where turbulent, low speed winds prevail [20,37]. Hybrid Savonius–Darrieus Systems Hybrid configurations co-axially combine a Savonius rotor (inner) with a Darrieus rotor (outer), leveraging the superior self-starting torque of the former to overcome Darrieus negative torque region at low TSR, whilst Darrieus generates the majority of power at operational speeds [1,4]. The geometric design must carefully balance the relative swept areas and solidity values to prevent Savonius drag from excessively degrading the overall Cp at higher TSR [17]. Numerous geometric variants—including offset Savonius buckets, partially ducted inner rotors, and axially separated stages—have been proposed in the literature [4,38]. Reported maximum Cp values for optimized hybrids fall in the range of 0.25–0.35, lower than standalone Darrieus designs but coupled with reliable self-starting from near-zero wind speed [1,38]. ### 3. Aerodynamic Fundamentals and Governing Parameters

Before introducing the non-dimensional governing parameters, the force and moment dynamics of a vertical axis rotor are summarized. As a Darrieus blade traverses its circular path of radius R with angular velocity ω, the relative velocity W(θ) seen by the blade is the vector sum of the local airflow velocity and the blade’s tangential velocity ωR; consequently both the magnitude of W and the local angle of attack α undergo continuous cyclic variation with the azimuthal position θ [26,36]. From the velocity triangle: W(θ)= V∞ rh (λ + cosθ)2 + sin2θ i ; α(θ) = arctan[sinθ/(λ + cosθ)], where λ = ωR/V∞is the tip speed ratio [36]. Resolving the resultant aerodynamic force along the blade-tangential and blade-normal directions yield the cyclic force components Ft(θ) = ½ρ c W2(θ) [CL sin α(θ) −CD cos α(θ)], Fn(θ) = ½ρ c W2(θ) [CL cos α(θ) + CD sin α(θ)], where Ft generates the shaft torque while Fn governs blade root and strut loading. The instantaneous torque produced by N blades is T(θ) = NRFt(θ). Because both W and α vary continuously with θ, the Ft (θ) waveform is strongly asymmetric over a single revolution. The maximum and minimum effective velocities occur near θ = 0◦and 180◦, where sin θ →0 and the angle of attack passes through zero; consequently Ft can become small or even negative in these regions [26]. In the upwind halfcycle the blade typically experiences positive tangential force as long as α(θ) remains below the static stall angle, producing the principal torque peak; in the downwind half-cycle the blade traverses the velocity deficit wake of the upwind blades, generating a smaller secondary peak [39]. At low tip speed ratios, the angle of attack excursion exceeds the static stall threshold, triggering dynamic stall with severe hysteresis in the Ft(θ) waveform

and consequent reductions in cycle averaged efficiency together with elevated structural vibration [36]. For drag-driven Savonius rotors, the situation is fundamentally different: Ft is generated not by aerofoil lift but by the asymmetric drag between the advancing concave bucket (high CD) and the returning convex bucket (low CD), and persists at every azimuth [39]. This continuous tangential force is the physical origin of the family’s reliable self-starting capability across all initial blade orientations.

#### 3.1. Tip Speed Ratio and Power Coefficient

TSR or λ is the primary non-dimensional parameter governing VAWT aerodynamic behaviour, defined as λ = ωR/V∞, where ω is the angular velocity (rad/s), R is the rotor radius (m), and V∞is the free stream wind velocity (m/s) [40]. For a Darrieus rotor, the instantaneous relative velocity experienced by a blade at azimuthal position θ is given by W(θ) = V∞ rh (λ + cosθ)2 + sin2θ i , whilst the instantaneous geometric angle of attack is α(θ) = arctan[sinθ/(λ + cosθ)]. These relations reveal that at low TSR, the blade experiences extreme angle of attack excursions (far exceeding static stall limits) during each rotation, precipitating dynamic stall and associated torque troughs [12,41]. At high TSR, the angle of attack excursions diminish and the rotor approaches a quasi-steady aerodynamic regime, but viscous losses and wake interference become dominant [31,42]. The aerodynamic power coefficient is defined as Cp = P 1/2ρV3 ∞A = Cmλ, where P is the extracted power (W), ρ is air density (kg/m3), A is the rotor swept area (m2), and Cm is the moment coefficient. The Betz limit of Cp,max = 16/27 ∼0.593 provides the theoretical upper bound for any wind turbine, though practical VAWTs achieve Cp values of 0.35–0.45 under optimized conditions due to blade drag, wake interference, tip vortex losses, and mechanical inefficiencies [40]. #### 3.2. Reynolds Number and Solidity Effects

The chord-based Reynolds number (Re_c = Wc/ν, where c is chord length and ν is kinematic viscosity of air) governs the boundary layer behaviour on VAWT blades and

critically determines the onset and character of flow separation and dynamic stall [12,43]. Micro VAWTs operating at chord Reynolds numbers below 105 are subject to laminar separation bubbles and highly nonlinear lift–drag characteristics that standard turbulence models struggle to predict [44,45]. At these scales, NACA profiles exhibit premature stall and markedly reduced lift–curve slopes relative to high Re_c behaviour, necessitating dedicated low Re_c airfoil families or purpose-designed blade sections [46,47]. Reynolds number sensitivity is further compounded by the cyclic variation in relative velocity W(θ), which means the effective Re_c varies by a factor of 2–5 during a single revolution [31]. Rotor solidity (σ = Nc/πD for Darrieus, or σ = Nc/R in the simpler formulation) acts as a principal control parameter for the TSR at which peak Cp occurs. High-solidity rotors (σ > 0.4) exhibit peak efficiency at lower TSR (λ ≈2–3) and higher self-starting torque, but suffer from pronounced blade–wake interaction: downstream blades encounter significantly decelerated and turbulent flow shed by upstream blades, reducing their effective angle of attack and aerodynamic loading [44,48]. Low solidity designs (σ < 0.2) achieve peak Cp at higher TSR (λ > 4) with reduced wake interaction but poor self-starting and increased structural loads from centrifugal forces at elevated rotational speeds [44]. Optimum solidity for a three-bladed H-rotor has been extensively studied and is generally reported in the range of σ = 0.25–0.35 for conventional NACA symmetric profiles [48], though the optimum shifts with blade profile, Reynolds number, and target operational TSR.

#### 3.3. Blade Profile, Pitch Angle, and Number of Blades

The choice of blade aerodynamic profile is among the most consequential geometric decisions in VAWT design. Symmetric NACA four-digit series (0012–0021) have been adopted as benchmarks, with thickness-to-chord ratio t/c influencing both the maximum lift coefficient and the critical dynamic stall angle [12,30]. Thicker profiles (t/c = 0.18–0.21) exhibit more gradual stall onset and higher Cm at low TSR, favouring self-starting; thinner profiles (t/c = 0.12–0.15) achieve higher lift-to-drag ratios at design TSR but stall more abruptly [30,45]. Cambered profiles—including NACA four-digit series with nonzero mean camber lines and purpose-designed profiles such as the DU06-W200 and SNL series—introduce asymmetric lift generation that can improve Cp in the downwind half of the rotation cycle [44,45]. However, camber also introduces a non-uniform torque distribution that may worsen ripple in certain configurations [49]. Fixed-pitch VAWT blades present the blade to the flow at a fixed angle relative to the chord rotor radius line. Non-zero fixed-pitch angles—typically in the range of −2◦+ 4◦ (toeing inward, negative; toeing outward, positive)—can be used to bias the effective angle of attack towards the upwind or downwind half of the rotation, offering modest Cp improvements (5–10%) at the design TSR at the cost of increased asymmetry in loading [50]. Variable pitch mechanisms, reviewed in Section 7, enable dynamic optimization of the blade angle throughout the azimuthal cycle. The number of blades (N) directly governs the torque ripple frequency (N harmonics per revolution) and the blade–wake interaction severity. Three-bladed configurations are generally favoured as the optimum compromise, producing a torque ripple with three peaks per revolution at manageable amplitude [11,34].

#### 3.4. Dynamic Stall, Vortex Shedding, and Blade–Wake Interaction

Dynamic stall is the defining unsteady aerodynamic phenomenon governing VAWT performance at low to moderate TSR. As a VAWT blade traverses the upwind half-cycle (azimuthal angles θ ∼0–180), the geometric angle of attack α(θ) increases from zero, exceeds the static stall angle, and continues to increase before reversing. During the pitch-up phase, the boundary layer remains attached beyond the static stall angle due to the un-

steady momentum of the leading edge boundary layer; a leading edge vortex (LEV) forms, grows, and eventually convects over the suction surface, causing a transient lift overshoot (dynamic stall overshoot) of up to 50% above the static maximum lift coefficient [41,51]. The subsequent shedding of this LEV causes abrupt lift loss, flow reversal, and a deep stall vortex that travels downstream in the turbine wake [41]. Ferreira et al. [41] provided landmark particle image velocimetry (PIV) visualization of dynamic stall on a single-bladed Darrieus model, directly imaging the LEV formation and shedding process for the first time with sufficient spatial resolution. Their results revealed that the LEV shed at θ ∼100–130 convects downstream and interacts with the blade during its downwind pass (θ ∼180–360), imposing an additional flow perturbation on the returning blade—the blade vortex interaction (BVI) mechanism. At low TSR, this BVI significantly reduces the effective Cp of the downwind sector, which is already degraded by the reduced free stream momentum due to energy extraction by the upwind blades [41,52]. For high-solidity rotors, blade–wake interaction within the rotor plane is further amplified because each blade must traverse the concentrated wakes shed by preceding blades. Vortex shedding frequency, characterized by the Strouhal number St = fc/W (where f is the shedding frequency), scales with blade chord and relative velocity, generating unsteady wake structures that impose periodic forcing on downstream blades [53,54]. Flow curvature effects—first identified by Migliore et al. [55]—arise because VAWT blades travel on a circular path, creating a virtual camber effect equivalent to the curvature of the relative streamlines. Corrections for flow curvature are particularly important for cambered blade sections and must be incorporated in any semi-analytical aerodynamic model to avoid systematic overprediction of Cp at low TSR [12,55].

#### 3.5. Aerodynamic Modelling Hierarchy

VAWT aerodynamic prediction methods occupy a hierarchy of increasing physical fidelity, computational cost, and predictive scope. The single streamtube model of Templin, treats the rotor as a uniform actuator disc and yields rapid first-order Cp estimates but cannot resolve azimuthal variation. The multiple streamtube model of Strickland introduces azimuthally discretized induction and recovers basic Cp–λ trends. The Double Multiple Streamtube (DMST) model of Paraschivoiu [56] introduces independent upwind and downwind induction factors, capturing blade–wake-induced asymmetry, and incorporates semi-empirical dynamic stall models to account for unsteady aerodynamic effects. Vortex lattice and free wake vortex methods resolve unsteady lift and basic wake geometry with computational costs that are typically orders of magnitude lower than blade-resolved CFD (often on the order of 102–103 CPU·h per operating point), but they require closure for viscous separation [57]. URANS simulations with k-ω SST or transition SST closures serve as the industry standard compromise for VAWT aerodynamic analysis, as they are the only models shown to produce reasonable agreement with experiments [58]. The transitional versions (SSTI, TSST) are explicitly recommended as the models of choice across operational regimes from dynamic stall to highly rotational flow [58]. In practical use, the accuracy of these URANS approaches is typically around ±5–10% at the turbine’s peak power coefficient but can degrade significantly under dynamic stall conditions [59]. While costs can vary by two orders of magnitude depending on the setup, these methods generally require on the order of 103–104 CPU hours per operating point. For enhanced accuracy across the full operational range, higher fidelity methods such as DES and DDES can be deployed; hybrid RANS-LES approaches have been shown to improve the prediction of separated and vortical flow structures in VAWTs [60]. When used with appropriate grid refinement, these scale-resolving methods often achieve around ±3–7% error, at a computational cost in the 104–105 CPU hour range.

The most computationally intensive approach, wall-resolved LES, is restricted by its cost scaling with approximately Re(9/5) (i.e., the number of grid points N∞Re1.9). This scaling originates from the need to resolve the near-wall viscous layer and makes wallresolved LES prohibitively expensive for full-scale VAWT simulations at chord Reynolds numbers of ∼106.

#### 3.6. Turbulence Intensity Effects on VAWT Aerodynamics

Free stream turbulence intensity (TI), defined as TI = σu/V∞, where σu is the streamwise velocity standard deviation, modulates VAWT performance through three coupled mechanisms. (i) Boundary layer transition. High inflow TI > 5% promotes early bypass transition of the blade boundary layer, suppressing the laminar separation bubbles (LSBs) that govern the Re_c–sensetive stall behaviour of smooth NACA profiles at chord Reynolds numbers below approximately 5 × 105 [39,61,62]. The suppression of LSBs non-linearities reduces the variability of the lift–curve slope and extends the linear lift range, with the constant lift–curve slope region enlarging as TI increases. The net aerodynamic consequence is a modest improvement in the cycle-averaged power coefficient Cp at low to moderate tip speed ratios, an effect that partially offsets the inherent small-scale Reynolds number penalty. Experimental studies have quantified this improvement as a power coefficient increase of up to 20% when TI rises from smooth (≈0.5%) to highly turbulent (≈15%) inflow conditions. (ii) Dynamic stall character. Free stream TI modulates the dynamic stall cycle on VAWT blades by entraining small-scale vortical structures into the boundary layer, which delays LEV formation and attenuates the lift overshoot characteristic of stall onset [63]. Recent 3D LES of an H-Darrieus rotor confirmed that turbulence significantly affects lift while leaving drag essentially unchanged [64]. The associated reduction in cyclic torque amplitude lowers fatigue loading, but at the cost of a small reduction in peak instantaneous lift available for power extraction in the optimum TSR regime [39]. (iii) Wake recovery. High inflow TI accelerates the wake recovery of a VAWT through enhanced turbulent mixing and entrainment of momentum [65,66]. Wind tunnel studies have shown that grid turbulence markedly improves wake replenishment [66], and the turbulence intensity of the incoming flow plays a primary role in determining the wake recovery rate [65]. Field experiments confirm this accelerated recovery in high TI environments, with the wake velocity deficit recovering within approximately 3–5 rotor diameters (D) from the rotor (≈x/D = 3.3–4.5) [67]. In contrast, numerical simulations under uniform, low turbulence inflow indicate that the velocity recovery distance needed for a VAWT can exceed 15D [68], highlighting the dramatic difference between controlled laboratory conditions and the turbulent atmospheric boundary layer. This discrepancy between the rapid wake re-energization observed in the field and the more persistent wake deficit in low turbulence wind tunnel experiments complicates the direct transfer of laboratory wake data to real world deployments. (iv) Implications for turbulence model selection. Two equation RANS closures (k–ω SST, realizable k–ε) require explicit specification of TI and turbulent length scale at the inlet boundary, and their predictive accuracy degrades when the inflow turbulence spectrum is anisotropic—as in urban canyons [69]. The four-equation transition SST (γ–Re_θ) model captures the bypass transition mechanism explicitly and is therefore the preferred URANS closure for TI > 5% [70]. For TI > 15% with strong anisotropy, hybrid RANS-LES (DDES) or LES with synthetic inflow generation (e.g., divergence-free synthetic eddy method, SEM) is required to reproduce the spectral characteristics of the incoming turbulence.

### 4. Critical Performance Analysis

#### 4.1. Power Coefficient and Aerodynamic Efficiency

A rigorous cross-comparison of VAWT aerodynamic performance requires careful attention to the definition of swept area and the normalization convention adopted, as inconsistencies in the literature have led to erroneous comparisons between configurations [32,42]. H-Darrieus rotors achieve the highest reported Cp values among all VAWT types: Rezaeiha et al. [31] reported Cp = 0.41 at λ = 4.5 for a two-bladed NACA 0018 H-rotor with σ = 0.12 via URANS simulation, in close agreement with the experimental measurements of Battisti, L. et al. [73] (Cp = 0.4 at λ = 4.5) obtained in a large-scale wind tunnel. Castelli et al. [26] obtained Cp = 0.423 at λ = 2.4 using k–ε realizable modelling, illustrating the turbulence model sensitivity. The performance gap between 2D URANS predictions and 3D simulations is typically 5–15%, attributable to tip vortex losses, strut parasitic drag, and spanwise flow that 2D models inherently neglect [32,74]. The Zanforlin and Nishino [75] study of closely spaced VAWT pairs demonstrated that counter-rotating turbines at optimal lateral spacing can achieve an effective Cp exceeding 0.44 per rotor—a 10–15% improvement over the isolated rotor—through mutual channel acceleration of the inter-turbine flow. Savonius rotors exhibit a fundamentally lower Cp ceiling (typically 0.15–0.25), constrained by the drag differential operating principle and the inherently high drag of the

returning bucket [1]. Hybrid configurations report maximum Cp in the range of 0.25–0.35, representing a compromise that reflects the energy extraction limitations imposed by the inner Savonius rotor’s drag at elevated TSR [4,38]. Helical Darrieus designs achieve Cp values comparable to H-rotors (0.30–0.40) with substantially reduced torque ripple coefficients (reduced by 40–60% relative to straight-blade equivalents) [18,35], making them particularly attractive for noise-sensitive applications.

#### 4.2. Self-Starting Capability

Self-starting capability—defined as the ability of a VAWT to accelerate from rest under the action of aerodynamic torque alone—is one of the most practically important and frequently cited weaknesses of Darrieus configurations [11,27]. At startup, the blade chord Reynolds number is extremely low (Re < 104 in micro VAWT applications) and the angle of attack sweeps through the full 360◦range during each revolution, producing negative aerodynamic torque in substantial azimuthal sectors due to blade drag exceeding lift contributions [12,26]. Blade profile selection significantly modulates self-starting capability: cambered profiles generate non-zero torque at zero incidence and thus exhibit superior self-starting [44,45], whilst high-solidity configurations (σ > 0.35) with three or more blades produce sufficient net torque at low TSR to self-start from a wider range of initial blade positions [76]. The variable pitch mechanism provides the most reliable self-starting augmentation.

#### 4.3. Torque Ripple and Structural Implications

Torque ripple—the cyclic variation in aerodynamic torque with azimuthal blade position—is an intrinsic feature of all VAWT configurations. For an N-bladed straight Darrieus rotor, the torque oscillation occurs at N times per revolution, with a ripple coefficient (peak-to-peak torque variation divided by mean torque) in the range of 0.5–1.5 for typical two- and three-bladed configurations under attached flow conditions [34]. Under dynamic stall conditions (low TSR), the ripple coefficient rises dramatically and the torque waveform becomes highly non-sinusoidal, imposing complex broadband forcing on the drivetrain [19]. Helical Darrieus geometries substantially reduce ripple by distributing the azimuthal torque peak across multiple chord positions simultaneously, yielding near-constant torque output for twist angles exceeding 90◦[11]. Beyond helical blade geometry, three further torque ripple mitigation strategies operate at different system levels. (i) Mass moment of inertia/flywheel coupling. Increasing the rotational inertia I of the rotor shaft generator assembly attenuates the angular velocity ripple through the dynamic relation ∆ω = ∆T/I; the addition of a purpose-designed flywheel can substantially smooth shaft speed without modifying blade aerodynamics, at the cost of increased startup time and additional structural mass [77,78]. This strategy is particularly attractive for grid connected installations, where electrical side smoothing through power electronics handles the residual ripple [77]. (ii) Blade count and azimuthal staggering. Increasing the number of blades from two to three raises the principal torque harmonic from 2Ωto 3Ωand significantly reduces ripple amplitude at modest peak Cp cost; Du et al. [79] confirmed in their review that three or more blades minimize torque ripple effects, a finding consistent with earlier studies by Consul et al. and McIntosh et al. cited therein. (iii) Multi-stage axial stacking. Two or three rotor stages stacked vertically with a fixed azimuthal offset (typically 360◦/(N·k) for k stages) achieve helical equivalent smoothing without blade twist [10]. The strategies are not mutually exclusive: commercial small-scale Darrieus designs typically combine three-bladed helical configurations with inertial coupling for robust torque smoothing in real urban deployment [17,77].

#### 4.4. Induction Effects and Wake Interference

Induction factor. The induction factor a originates in one dimensional axial momentum theory, where it quantifies the fractional reduction in the axial flow velocity at the rotor plane relative to the undisturbed free stream: Vdisc = V∞(1 −a) [40]. Applying conservation of mass and momentum across an idealized actuator disc placed in steady, inviscid, incompressible, and unbounded flow yields the far wake velocity Vwake = V∞(1 −2a) and the power coefficient Cp = 4a(1 −a)2, which reaches its maximum value of 16/27 ≈0.593 at a = 1/3—the Betz limit [40,80]. This limit therefore holds only under the actuator disc idealizations and ceases to apply, in particular, when the rotor is operated in a confined or accelerated flow (wind tunnel blockage, diffuser augmentation, or array channel acceleration), conditions under which a rotor’s apparent Cp referenced to its swept area can exceed 16/27 without violating global energy conservation [81,82]. For VAWTs the actuator disc idealization is insufficient because the rotor occupies a finite streamwise depth (the swept rectangle D × H) and each blade traverses both the upwind and downwind halves of the rotor. Paraschivoiu’s [9] DMST model addresses this asymmetry by introducing two distinct induction factors—au for the upwind half and ad for the downwind half—coupled in series so that the velocity at the upwind rotor plane is Vdisc,u = V∞(1 −au), the velocity entering the downwind half (i.e., the wake of the upwind blade) is Ve = V∞(1 −2au), and the velocity at the downwind rotor plane is Vdisc,d = Ve(1 −ad) = V∞(1 −2au)(1 −ad) [26].The resulting strong asymmetry between au and ad directly explains the lower torque contribution of the downwind half observed in experimental and CFD studies of operating VAWTs [26], and is the physical basis for the wake deficit treatment underlying all subsequent semi-analytical VAWT performance models. Wake formation and structure. The VAWT wake is intrinsically three-dimensional and asymmetric, formed by three coupled vorticity sources: (i) spanwise shed vorticity released as the bound circulation Γ(θ) varies azimuthally over each revolution [41]; (ii) tip vortices rolled up at the blade ends owing to the spanwise pressure differential [52]; and (iii) dynamic stall vortices convected downstream from the upwind blade passage at low TSR [51]. These mechanisms produce a near-wake region composed of counterrotating vortex rows in the rotor mid plane and counter-rotating tip vortex pairs at the rotor extremities, with the wake bulk laterally skewed by 5–10◦relative to the free stream direction owing to asymmetric momentum extraction between the upwind and downwind halves [83]. Energy deficit and recovery. The cycle-averaged streamwise velocity deficit immediately downstream of the rotor reaches 30–50% of V∞for an isolated VAWT operating near peak Cp [84]. Wake recovery proceeds in three stages: a near-wake region (0–3D) dominated by coherent vortex shedding; a transition region (3–8D) in which Kelvin Helmholtzand Crow-type instabilities break down the coherent structures, generating broadband turbulence; and a far wake (>8D) with a self-similar Gaussian profile recovering by ambient turbulent diffusion [85]. Critically, the bluff-body-like character of the VAWT wake (compared to the actuator disc behaviour of HAWTs) enables vertical entrainment of high momentum fluid from above the canopy, producing recovery rates roughly twice those of equivalent HAWTs for closely spaced arrays [86]. Mitigation—blade design level. Optimal solidity (σ = 0.20–0.30 for three-bladed Hrotors) improves performance and wake characteristics [87], while fixed-blade pitch offsets dramatically accelerate near-wake recovery through enhanced advection [88]. Helical blade geometries spread vortex shedding across a broader frequency range and yield shorter near-wake regions [89]. Cambered airfoils decrease the force hysteresis cycle during deep

dynamic stall, thereby reducing wake perturbations, and leading edge protuberances effectively subdue dynamic stall effects [90]. Mitigation—array level. Counter-rotating VAWT pairs at lateral spacing of 1.0–1.5D produce channel acceleration between the rotors and wake contraction in the downwind path, yielding 10–17% power coefficient gains per turbine relative to isolated operation [91,92]. The sense of rotation is critical: configurations in which the upwind moving blades are located at the centre of the pair produce a narrow wake and a power improvement, whereas the opposite arrangement—with downwind moving blades adjacent—degrades performance and results in a wide wake deficit [93]. At the farm scale, counter-rotating VAWT arrays can exploit the vertical flux of turbulent kinetic energy from the atmospheric surface layer to extract energy from above the farm, enabling power densities an order of magnitude greater than conventional HAWT farms [94]. Mitigation—active flow control. Blade-mounted active flow control pulsed plasma actuators, blowing/suction slots, synthetic jets, and dual synthesis jets—effectively suppresses dynamic stall on VAWT blades [95,96]. The blowing/suction scheme eliminates negative torque at low to medium tip speed ratios; closed loop optimization of synthetic jet arrays raises peak Cp by 43% (∼17% net gain after deducting actuator energy cost) [96,97]. Deep reinforcement learning, capable of autonomously discovering optimal actuator schedules, is an emerging frontier for further gains [97].

#### 4.5. Cross-Study Performance Trends

A meta-comparison across the cited URANS and experimental studies establishes three robust trends. First, peak Cp for an H-Darrieus rotor scales weakly with solidity in the optimum band (σ ≈0.20–0.35) and is almost independent of the number of blades within this regime; performance degrades at higher solidity due to increased blockage and blade-to-blade interactions that substantially alter the effective angle of attack [29,98]. Second, peak Cp exhibits a monotonic improvement with chord-based Reynolds number at least up to Re_c ≈4.2 × 105—the highest value studied—with turbine performance and wake remaining Re dependent over this entire range [29]. Third, the optimal tip speed ratio λopt is not given by a constant product with solidity; rather, λopt is invariant to the newly introduced parameter σλ3, confirming that the relative-velocity-induced angle of attack envelope must remain bounded by the static stall angle for attached flow operation [29]. These trends provide a robust empirical envelope against which any new VAWT configuration should be benchmarked. The internal aerodynamics of VAWTs are dominated by the strong momentum deficit created in the downwind half of the rotor disc by energy extraction in the upwind sector. This self-induced velocity reduction, formally captured by the induction factor a, significantly reduces the effective angle of attack and relative velocity experienced by downwind blades [99]. The far wake of a VAWT recovers over a distance of 10–20 rotor diameters downstream, slower than HAWT wakes of equivalent power, but the broader lateral wake footprint characteristic of VAWTs enables tighter VAWT arrays compared to HAWT arrays, with higher power density per land area [75,100]—a finding that has attracted significant interest for urban and offshore floating platform applications.

### 5. Numerical Modelling and Simulation Approaches

#### 5.1. Computational Fluid Dynamics Methodologies

#### 5.1.1. RANS and URANS Approaches

Steady RANS treats the rotor as a time-averaged source/sink—most commonly through actuator line, actuator surface, or multiple reference frame formulations—and is computationally inexpensive (≈102–103 CPU·h per operating point). It provides useful

first-order Cp estimates for cycle-averaged performance in attached flow regimes (λ > 3 for typical H-rotors), supports rapid parametric design exploration, and converges robustly with minimal mesh requirements. Its principal limitations are intrinsic: the time-averaging assumption removes all phase information, so dynamic stall, blade–vortex interaction, torque ripple, and the asymmetric upwind/downwind loading characteristic of VAWTs cannot be resolved. Unsteady RANS (URANS)—solving the ensemble-averaged equations with a timemarching scheme using sliding mesh or overset (Chimera) approaches—has become the dominant CFD methodology in the VAWT literature, offering a tractable compromise between cost and physical fidelity [26,31,50]. The choice of turbulence closure within URANS is non-trivial. The k-ω SST model is the workhorse for design stage simulations because of its good wall-bounded performance and adverse pressure gradient behaviour, with reported ±5–10% Cp accuracy at peak operation when validated against experimental benchmarks [26]. However, the SST model is calibrated for fully turbulent attached flow, and its predictive accuracy degrades in the deep stall regime characteristic of low λ VAWT operation, where DES variants based on the same SST closure have been shown to be markedly superior [101]. The four-equation transition SST (γ–Re_θ) model, which transports an intermittency variable to capture laminar to turbulent transition, has been benchmarked against the standard SST in the VAWT specific comparison of Rezaeiha et al. [58]: across three experimental datasets and seven candidate eddy viscosity closures, transition SST and SSTI delivered the best overall agreement with experimental power and wake data. For VAWT wake prediction in array contexts, transition SST has likewise been identified as the closest URANS approximation to experimental near and far wake measurements [102]. Beyond closure selection, Rezaeiha et al. [42] established the URANS methodological best practice envelope: domain extents of at least 15D upstream, 10D downstream, and 20D total width; rotating sub-domain diameter Drot ≈1.5D; azimuthal time step ∆θ ≤0.1◦at low λ; and a minimum of 20 revolutions before sampling for periodic convergence. Despite these advances, URANS retains intrinsic limitations: 2D simulations systematically over predict Cp by 30–50% relative to validated 3D simulations because of the omission of tip vortex losses and strut drag [103]; and even 3D URANS struggles to resolve the phase resolved leading edge vortex shedding cycle and the broadband wake turbulence characteristic of VAWT operation [104].

#### 5.1.2. LES and DES Approaches

Large eddy simulation (LES) directly resolves the energy-containing turbulent eddies and models only the sub-grid scale motions, providing substantially higher physical fidelity than URANS for separated and transitional flows characteristic of VAWT operation [29,49]. LES of VAWTs has produced detailed insights unavailable from URANS: Posa et al. [104] performed wall-resolved LES of a single H-Darrieus rotor, identifying KelvinHelmholtz instabilities of the bounding shear layers as the dominant transition mechanism in the nearwake region; Abkar and Dabiri [105] conducted LES of a Darrieus rotor and demonstrated that the velocity profile in the central part of the wake exhibits self-similarity, supporting the application of analytical wake recovery models; Peng et al. [64] performed a 3D LES of an H-Darrieus rotor at varying turbulence intensities and found that turbulence significantly modulates the lift component while exerting negligible influence on drag. LES of coupled VAWT configurations by Posa [106] further established that bluff-body-like wake mixing accelerates downstream momentum recovery, supporting the closely spaced array concept. The principal limitation of LES is computational cost: wall-resolved approaches scale approximately as R(9/5) e , rendering them prohibitive at full chord Reynolds numbers (Re_c ≈106), while wall-modelled LES reduces cost at a moderate accuracy penalty.

DES and its delayed and improved variants (DDES, IDDES) provide engineering compromises between URANS and LES, applying URANS in attached boundary layers and transitioning to LES in separated shear layers and wakes. Wang et al. [101] demonstrated that DES based on the SST k–ω closure is superior to URANS for predicting deep dynamic stall on a sinusoidally pitching NACA 0012 at Re ≈105—a regime closely analogous to small to medium VAWT operation. Lei et al. [107] applied 3D IDDES to a two-bladed VAWT and resolved the spanwise distribution of dynamic stall vortex shedding with quantitative agreement to experimental torque. Sheidani et al. [108] benchmarked URANS against LES for VAWT wake prediction, showing that URANS reasonably reproduces mean wake quantities but underestimates wake turbulence in the near-wake region. Kuang et al. [109] applied IDDES to floating VAWT pairs and quantified the influence of platform pitch motion on downstream rotor wake recovery—a study unfeasible at LES cost but beyond the resolution of steady RANS. Lam and Peng [110] complemented this body of work by directly comparing 2D and 3D URANS against IDDES for an H-Darrieus rotor, finding that 3D transition SST provided wake predictions comparable to IDDES at a substantially lower cost. Collectively, this literature has established LES and hybrid RANS-LES as the high-fidelity reference against which URANS closures are calibrated and validated, while URANS remains the design stage workhorse.

#### 5.2. Turbulence Model Evaluation

The selection of turbulence closure model is the dominant source of systematic error in URANS based VAWT simulations. The k–ω SST model has become the widely accepted standard following the systematic validation work of Rezaeiha et al. [31,50], yielding Cp accuracy typically within ±5–10% of experimental values at the peak TSR region. The four-equation transition SST model (γ–Reθ) incorporates intermittency transport to predict laminar turbulent transition location, substantially improving Cp prediction accuracy at low to moderate TSR where transitional effects are significant [19,43]. Zhao et al. [19] demonstrated that the transition SST model reduced Cp error from 12% 4% against experiment for dynamic stall conditions, with only 15–20% additional computational overhead above k–ω SST. #### 5.3. Mesh Strategy and Simulation Setup

VAWT URANS simulations typically employ a sliding mesh approach in which the rotor is enclosed within a circular rotating sub-domain coupled to the stationary outer domain through a non-conformal interface [31]. The rotating sub-domain diameter Drot must place the interface outside the principal high shear zones of the blade induced flow; the systematic study of Rezaeiha et al. [31] showed that varying Drot between 1.25D and 2.0D produced Cp differences below 1%, with Drot ≈1.5D recommended as the optimal compromise between accuracy and computational cost. In 3D simulations, the sub-domain

height should exceed the rotor height by at least one chord length above and below the blade tips (Hrot ≥H + 2c) to avoid clipping the tip vortices at the interface [114]. The global outer domain must extend far enough to avoid boundary-induced perturbation of the rotor flow. Rezaeiha et al. [42] established minimum extents of 15D upstream, 10D downstream, and 20D total width (≈10D lateral on each side) for converged Cp prediction across a range of tip speed ratios and solidities. The associated blockage ratio β = Arotor/Across should remain below 5% for first-order predictions and below 3% for benchmark quality validation, since wind tunnel sections operating at β ≈5–10% require explicit blockage corrections (e.g., Maskell or Pope–Harper) to recover the equivalent free stream Cp [115]. Numerical diffusion at the rotating stationary interface is minimized by matching cell sizes within ≈±20% across the interface and employing a conservative flux interpolation scheme [116]. Spatial discretization has a strong influence on VAWT CFD accuracy. Rezaeiha et al. [42] performed 110 high-fidelity two-dimensional URANS simulations of vertical axis wind turbines across tip speed ratios of 1.5–5.5 and solidities of 0.06–0.24, establishing guidelines for temporal resolution, domain sizing, and convergence. An azimuthal increment of ∆θ = 0.1◦was recommended for λ ≤3.5 and ∆θ = 0.5◦for higher ratios, with minimum domain distances of 15D upstream, 10D downstream, and 20D laterally. A minimum of 20–30 revolutions was required before sampling to ensure power coefficient convergence. The structured quadrilateral mesh (~400,000 cells) employed the transition SST (γ–θ) turbulence model with y+ insensitive wall treatment, yielding a fine grid GCI of 0.85%. The authors cautioned that although individual parameter sensitivities remain below 1%, their cumulative effect can be significant. Three-dimensional simulations demonstrated that 2D simulations systematically over predict Cp by 15–30% for rotors with H/D < 2 [74], validating the necessity of 3D modelling for low aspect ratio designs.

#### 5.4. Semi-Analytical Models

The DMST, as formulated by Paraschivoiu et al. [56], incorporates distinct upwind and downwind induction to capture the momentum deficit effect on downstream blades. However, all streamtube models use quasi-steady aerodynamic coefficients derived from static airfoil polars, fundamentally neglecting dynamic stall and flow curvature effects [12]. The vortex lattice method (VLM) and free wake vortex models offer improved fidelity by explicitly tracking vortex filaments, capturing unsteady lift and basic wake geometry [12]. These models remain useful for rapid parametric studies but require CFD validation for final performance prediction.

#### 5.5. Machine-Learning-Augmented Turbulence Modelling

An emerging and rapidly growing frontier in VAWT aerodynamic prediction is the integration of machine learning (ML) techniques with conventional turbulence closure models. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), neural network [117], and augmented Reynolds stress model (ARSM) have demonstrated the capability to learn correction terms for standard RANS closures using high-fidelity LES or DNS training data, potentially bridging the accuracy gap between URANS and LES at a fraction of the computational cost. Deep reinforcement learning has been explored for active flow control optimization on VAWT blades, with agents learning optimal blowing/suction schedules to delay LEV shedding and reduce torque fluctuations. Küppers, J.-P et al. [118] used a WaveNet deep neural network (a causal dilated convolutional architecture) trained on the Ohio State University (OSU) experimental dataset for the S809 wind turbine airfoil undergoing pitch oscillations. It predicts lift, drag, and

pitching moment coefficients—including cycle-to-cycle stochastic variations, nonlinear hysteresis, force peaks, and secondary vortex effects characteristic of deep dynamic stall. These ML-augmented approaches are still in early development for rotating machinery applications, but represent a paradigm shift in VAWT performance prediction methodology.

#### 5.6. Limitations of Existing Numerical Studies

A critical reading of the VAWT CFD literature reveals four recurring methodological shortcomings that complicate inter study comparison and reproducibility. (i) Twodimensional simulations remain dominant despite a documented 30–50% overprediction of peak Cp relative to validated 3D simulations, attributable to the omission of blade tip vortex losses and strut parasitic drag effects particularly pronounced at low aspect ratio (H/D < 2) [103,107]. (ii) Single turbulence closure studies, performed without sensitivity analysis across at least two models (e.g., k–ω SST and transition SST), propagate model bias as physical conclusions in dynamic stall regimes [119]. (iii) Grid convergence index (GCI) reporting via Richardson extrapolation is absent in many older studies, complicating discrimination between physical and numerical effects [120]. (iv) Laboratory-scale Reynolds numbers (Re_c = 104–105) typically fall one to two orders of magnitude below those of operational VAWTs (Re_c = 105–106), yet this mismatch is rarely quantified [91]. Minimum reporting standards should therefore include: 3D simulations for low aspect ratio rotors, sensitivity to at least two turbulence closures, GCI-based discretization error, and explicit Reynolds number documentation.

### 6. Experimental Investigations

#### 6.1. Wind Tunnel Studies

Wind tunnel experimentation has provided the definitive validation dataset for VAWT CFD models. The landmark PIV study of Ferreira et al. [41] employed a single-bladed Darrieus model in a closed section wind tunnel with a NACA 0015 airfoil (chord 0.05 m, rotor diameter 0.4 m) at TU Delft’s low turbulence wind tunnel. They captured phase-locked velocity and vorticity fields across the full blade rotation at three tip speed ratios (λ = 2, 3, 4) and two Reynolds numbers (Re ≈50, 000 and 70, 000). The paper’s central contribution is a detailed characterization of the leading-edge separation vortex—its formation at roughly θ = 72◦, two-phase growth pattern, and detachment at θ ≈138◦along with trailing edge vorticity dynamics and blade vortex interaction during the downwind passage. Battisti et al. [73] conducted a comprehensive experimental campaign on both H-Darrieus and troposkein VAWT models in a large-scale wind tunnel (test section 4 m × 4 m), revealing a consistent 10–15% Cp overprediction by URANS simulations in the peak Cp region.

#### 6.2. Field Measurements and Urban Deployment

Field measurements of VAWTs in urban environments are considerably more challenging than controlled wind tunnel experiments due to the stochastic nature of urban wind: TI typically exceeds 15–25% at rooftop height, directional variability is high, and mean wind speeds are often below 5 m/s for extended periods [45]. Balduzzi et al. [33] assessed the feasibility of installing an H-Darrieus VAWT on building rooftops in an urban environment through a CFD-based methodology. The study characterized the wind flow field over buildings with varying geometrical proportions and developed a correction model to account for the skewed and turbulent inflow conditions typical of rooftop installations. By combining the aerodynamic analysis with an annual energy yield estimation, the authors demonstrated that building geometry and turbine positioning relative to surrounding structures critically influence energy producibility. The study highlighted the suitability of Darrieus VAWTs for urban deployment owing to their omnidirectional operation, low visual im-

pact, and tolerance to skewed flows, while underscoring that accurate site-specific flow characterization remains essential for reliable performance predictions. Danao et al. [112] showed that wind speed fluctuations exceeding 10% amplitude at low frequencies degrade VAWT performance by promoting deep dynamic stall, whereas smaller, higher frequency fluctuations preserve a cycle-averaged Cp comparable to steady conditions.

#### 6.3. Reynolds Number Scaling and Experimental Uncertainty

A persistent challenge in VAWT experimental research is the Reynolds number gap between laboratory scale models (Re = 104–105) and full-scale turbines (Re = 105–106). In this range, airfoil aerodynamic characteristics are strongly Re-dependent, particularly for symmetric NACA profiles where laminar separation bubbles dominate boundary layer behaviour below Re ≈5 × 105 [30,45]. Bianchini et al. [45] reported that experimental uncertainty in airfoil polars increases substantially at low Reynolds numbers—reaching approximately 10% at Re = 6 × 104 compared to 3% at Re ≥1.5 × 105—and emphasized that VAWT performance predictions are highly sensitive to the accuracy of input blade data, making Reynolds number-matched aerodynamic datasets essential for reliable modelling of small-scale Darrieus turbines. ### 7. Design Optimization and Performance Enhancement Strategies

#### 7.1. Blade Shape Optimization

Blade profile optimization represents perhaps the most thoroughly investigated avenue for VAWT performance improvement. Mohamed et al. [2] coupled URANS simula-

tions with an optimization procedure to determine the optimal blade shape (skeleton line) of a Savonius turbine operating with an obstacle plate shielding the returning blade, demonstrating a significant increase in power output compared to the conventional semi-circular bucket design. Chan et al. [25] coupled a genetic algorithm with 2D URANS simulations to optimize the Savonius blade cross-section, achieving up to 33% improvement in Cp over the conventional semi-circular design at TSR = 0.8, with the optimized turbine outperforming the baseline across a wide range of tip speed ratios (0.6–1.2). Bedon et al. [44] developed a multi-objective genetic optimization framework (NSGA-II) coupled with 2D URANS CFD to evolve a new airfoil shape (WUP 1615) for Darrieus wind turbines, achieving an 8% increase in peak power coefficient over the NACA 0018 baseline, with the optimized profile exhibiting higher lift at positive angles of attack but a more abrupt stall behaviour at low tip speed ratios.

#### 7.2. Variable Pitch Mechanisms

Variable pitch mechanisms dynamically adjust the blade pitch angle β(θ) as a function of azimuthal position. Kirke and Lazauskas [27] reviewed the inherent limitations of fixed-pitch Darrieus hydrokinetic turbines—namely poor self-starting capability, low efficiency due to blade stall at low tip speed ratios, and structural shaking from cyclic force variations—and argued that variable pitch offers the potential to simultaneously achieve high starting torque, improved efficiency, and reduced vibration, though no simple and robust pitch control system had yet been demonstrated. Passive variable pitch systems— using aerodynamic, centrifugal, or spring loaded mechanisms—represent a compromise solution with reported Cp improvements of 8–12% over fixed-pitch baselines [27,56].

#### 7.3. Flow Control and Augmentation Devices

Guide vanes redirect and accelerate the approaching wind towards the powerproducing upwind sector, reporting Cp improvements of 5–12% in controlled experiments [111]. Diffuser shrouds create a lower pressure region at the rotor plane through Bernoulli acceleration, increasing the effective wind velocity and enabling Cp values exceeding the Betz limit when expressed relative to the rotor swept area (though not relative to the total device area) [121]. Active flow control techniques—including leading edge suction, trailing edge blowing, plasma actuators, and vortex generators—have been investigated as methods to delay dynamic stall. Liu et al. [122] proposed a blowing suction synergy (BSS) active flow control strategy for an H-type Darrieus VAWT, in which leading edge blowing and trailing edge suction are applied to the blade surface, demonstrating effective suppression of flow separation and vortex shedding, elimination of negative torque regions, and a significant improvement in aerodynamic and aeroacoustic performance compared to the uncontrolled baseline.

#### 7.4. Hybridization Strategies

The Savonius–Darrieus hybrid configuration is the most extensively studied strategy for addressing the self-starting limitation. Chegini et al. [72] numerically investigated the self-starting capability of a Darrieus–Savonius hybrid VAWT and showed that coupling the two rotors improved self-starting performance with a 26.9% increase in Cp at the lowest TSR, while at higher TSR the Savonius component degraded overall efficiency due to its limited aerodynamic performance—a trade-off that was partially mitigated by front and side deflectors, which improved peak Cp by up to 30% at the operational TSR of 2.6. Recent hybrid designs incorporating helical Savonius inner rotors with three-bladed straight H-Darrieus outer rotors have demonstrated improved performance consistency across the full wind speed spectrum [35].

#### 7.5. Array and System-Level Optimization

Unlike HAWT arrays, where inter-turbine spacing must be large (8–10D), counterrotating VAWT pairs at lateral spacings of 1.0–1.5D can generate channel acceleration between rotors that benefits both turbines simultaneously [75,100]. Zanforlin and Nishino [75] performed comprehensive 2D URANS simulations of counter-rotating VAWT pairs and identified two key mechanisms for enhanced power generation: lateral velocity suppression in the upwind path and wake contraction in the downwind path, achieving power gains of up to 17% at the optimal TSR for side-by-side arrangements. They further showed that staggered configurations cannot surpass side-by-side pairs, and that a spacing of 3D between turbine axes offers the best compromise across varying wind directions—findings with direct implications for the design of closely spaced VAWT farms with significantly higher power density than conventional HAWT layouts. Kinzel et al. [100] performed field measurements on an 18-turbine counter-rotating VAWT array and demonstrated that the planform turbulent kinetic energy flux entering from above the canopy (∼22 Wm−2) was sufficient to fully replenish the power extracted by each turbine pair, confirming that closely spaced counter-rotating VAWT arrays can be sustained by vertical energy transport—a finding that supports an order of magnitude higher power density per unit land area compared to conventional HAWT farms and provides a physical basis for optimizing VAWT farm layouts around turbine pairing, spacing, and rotational sense rather than wake avoidance alone. #### 7.6. Comparative Aerodynamic Analysis: Straight vs. Helical Darrieus Blades

The choice between a straight (H-rotor) and a helical (twisted) Darrieus blade configuration involves a multi-dimensional engineering trade-off whose principal axes are summarized below. (i) Azimuthal load distribution and torque ripple. In a straight-bladed N-rotor, all blades simultaneously occupy identical azimuthal phases, producing a torque waveform with N peaks per revolution and a large peak-to-peak ripple coefficient ζ = (Tmax −Tmin)/T characteristic of attached flow operation [36,73]. A helical blade with twist angle Φ continuously distributes the azimuthal phase along the blade span; for Φ ≥360◦/N (≈120◦for a three-bladed rotor), the integrated torque becomes nearly constant, with reported reductions of up to ∼85% in the cyclic torque variation relative to a straight-bladed equivalent at the same operating point [32,123]. Parametric studies of helix angle confirm that the minimum torque coefficient deviation is achieved near Φ = 120◦for three-bladed configurations, while peak average torque is typically obtained at intermediate angles (60–90◦), reflecting a trade-off between ripple smoothing and cycle-averaged efficiency [124]. (ii) Cycle-averaged Cp. The helical geometry imposes a small aerodynamic penalty under steady inflow: the spanwise twist introduces a continuous variation in effective angle of attack along the blade, reducing the peak instantaneous lift relative to a straight blade at the same azimuthal phase and lowering the cycle-averaged Cp at the same σ and λ [36]. The penalty is configuration-dependent and grows with TSR. Moradi Ghareghani et al. [125] reported that a straight-bladed (3-PB) configuration produces 6% more average torque than its helical equivalent at TSR = 0.44, rising to over 150% at TSR = 1.77, reflecting the larger deficit of helical rotors at high λ where straight blades operate closest to their attached flow optimum. Helix angle sweeps confirm a trade-off between cycle-averaged torque (maximized near Φ = 60◦) and torque smoothness (maximized near Φ = 120◦) [124]. Under unsteady or turbulent inflow, however, the helical geometry’s gentler load gradients reduce dynamic stall susceptibility, and Scheurich and Brown [123] demonstrated that an optimally curved and twisted helical rotor can in fact generate more power than a straight-bladed equivalent at constant rotational speed. (iii) Structural and aeroelastic implications. The smooth azimuthal load distribution of the helical configuration eliminates the broadband forcing of the drivetrain at NΩand its harmonics, materially reducing fatigue accumulation at the blade–strut junctions, the main shaft, and the bearing system [125,126]. The reduced cyclic loading translates into lower fatigue-driven sizing constraints on the drivetrain components and supports a longer service life [127]. Counteracting this benefit, the helical blade geometry introduces a more complex aeroelastic coupling: spanwise varying centrifugal and aerodynamic loads modify the modal stiffness and natural frequencies of the rotating blade, requiring a careful coupled CFD–FEM analysis to ensure that operating speeds remain bounded away from flutter and resonance margins—an issue particularly acute for slender, high-aspect-ratio blades [128]. (iv) Aeroacoustic signature. Straight-bladed rotors generate characteristic blade passage tones at the blade passage frequency (NΩ) and its harmonics, along with broadband noise contributions from dynamic stall effects and trailing edge self-noise [129,130]. Helical blade configurations disrupt this coherent tonal signature by smearing the azimuthal source distribution. A numerical study of helical Darrieus turbines demonstrated a 20 dB reduction in the tonal peak at the first blade passage frequency for a high helix angle (120◦) rotor, alongside a decrease in broadband noise [131]. This significant tonal reduction makes such designs particularly relevant for deployments in noise-sensitive built environments [132]. (v) Manufacturability and cost. Straight Darrieus blades benefit from constant section geometry, allowing continuous low-cost production by pultrusion or extrusion of

composite profiles, with reported cost reductions of approximately 50% relative to conventional aluminum spar blades [133]. Helical blades, by contrast, require either custommoulded composite layups in multi-part moulds or, for prototype quantities, additive manufacturing—both approaches incurring substantially higher tooling and labour costs per unit [36]. The resulting cost premium represents a documented barrier to broad smallscale (<10 kW) commercial adoption of helical configurations despite their structural and aeroacoustic benefits [17]. ### 8. Conclusions and Future Research Perspectives

#### 8.1. Synthesis of Principal Findings

This review has provided a systematic, critical analysis of vertical axis wind turbine technology across the dimensions of aerodynamic theory, design geometry, performance metrics, numerical modelling, experimental investigation, and optimization strategy. The following principal conclusions emerge: First, the aerodynamic performance of VAWTs is governed by a complex interplay of TSR, solidity, Reynolds number, and blade geometry, mediated through unsteady flow phenomena—dynamic stall, LEV shedding, and blade–wake interaction—that defy quasi-steady analytical treatment. H-Darrieus configurations achieve the highest peak Cp (0.35–0.45), whilst Savonius rotors excel in self-starting and low wind performance but are limited to Cp ≤0.25 [1,11,31]. Hybrid configurations represent a viable engineering compromise, and helical geometries address torque ripple at the cost of manufacturing complexity [18,35]. Second, URANS with k–ω SST turbulence closure provides an adequate first-order prediction (Cp within ±5–10% of experimental benchmarks in the peak TSR region) at acceptable computational cost [31,42]. For dynamic stall-dominated regimes (λ < 2.5), transition SST and DES/LES approaches offer meaningfully superior fidelity [19,49]. The

quantitative influence of turbulence model choice on predicted Cp can exceed 15%, underscoring the critical importance of rigorous model selection and validation. Third, design optimization strategies collectively demonstrate that achievable peak Cp improvements of 15–30% above baseline are physically realizable, but require simultaneous multi-objective optimization accounting for self-starting, torque ripple, structural loading, and operational wind speed distribution [27,44,48]. No single enhancement strategy has yet emerged as universally superior across all performance metrics.

#### 8.2. Identified Research Gaps and Future Directions

Turbulence modelling for unsteady and transitional flows: The accurate prediction of dynamic stall onset, LEV evolution, and post-stall reattachment at low to moderate chord Reynolds numbers remains the foremost unsolved modelling challenge. Datadriven turbulence modelling approaches—incorporating machine learning corrections trained on high-fidelity LES or experimental databases—represent a promising emerging direction. PINNs that embed the Navier–Stokes equations as soft constraints during training have demonstrated improved generalization to unseen operating conditions, but their application to the strongly periodic, separated flows of VAWT blades requires dedicated validation campaigns [19,43]. Aeroelastic coupling and structural dynamics: The coupled aerodynamic–structural response of VAWT blades under combined centrifugal, gravitational, and periodic aerodynamic loading has received comparatively little attention. Blade flutter, fatigue crack propagation at blade–strut junctions, and the influence of aeroelastic deformation on effective angle of attack distribution are priority areas, particularly for large-scale VAWTs and innovative flexible or morphing blade designs [10,134]. Urban wind field characterization and site-specific optimization: High-fidelity urban CFD simulations coupled with VAWT aerodynamic models are needed to develop sitespecific optimization tools, accounting for the full joint probability distribution of wind speed and direction rather than mean speed only metrics [33,35,121]. Floating offshore VAWT platforms: The application of VAWTs to floating offshore wind platforms has emerged as a transformative research direction. The inherent advantages of VAWTs for floating platforms—lower centre of gravity, reduced overturning moments, absence of yaw mechanisms susceptible to marine corrosion, and tolerance of non-uniform inflow conditions from platform motion—position them as strong candidates for deep water offshore wind deployment. Coupled aero-hydro-servo-elastic modelling of floating VAWT systems, accounting for platform pitch and heave motions on blade aerodynamics, represents a major interdisciplinary research frontier. Integration with building energy systems and green hydrogen: The integration of building integrated VAWTs with photovoltaic arrays, battery storage, and hydrogen electrolysis systems offers pathways towards net-zero energy buildings. Cross-disciplinary research bridging aerodynamics, energy systems engineering, and building physics is urgently needed [35,121]. Noise generation mechanisms and mitigation: VAWT aeroacoustics—including trailing edge noise, leading edge turbulence interaction noise, and bluff-body wake noise from struts—has received far less systematic investigation than HAWT noise. The coupled aeroelastic–aeroacoustic response, wherein structural vibration modes interact with unsteady aerodynamic loading to amplify or modify noise radiation patterns, represents a particularly important but under explored area [35,135].

#### 8.3. Recommendations for Future Work

Based on the identified research gaps, the following specific recommendations for future research are offered: (i) development and validation of transition-sensitive RANS models specifically calibrated on VAWT dynamic stall datasets at chord Reynolds numbers 104–106 using matched PIV and surface pressure measurement campaigns; (ii) high-fidelity (LES/DDES) three-dimensional simulations of full VAWT rotors including struts, shaft, and support structure to quantify parasitic drag contributions; (iii) experimental and numerical investigation of VAWT aeroelastic behaviour using fluid–structure interaction (FSI) solvers; (iv) development of urban optimized blade profiles using multi-objective optimization frameworks; (v) systematic investigation of VAWT array configurations in realistically modelled urban canyons; (vi) integration of machine learning surrogate models into VAWT optimization workflows; (vii) coupled aero-hydro-elastic analysis of floating offshore VAWT platforms under realistic sea state conditions; and (viii) experimental aeroacoustic characterization campaigns using microphone arrays and beam-forming techniques to identify and rank VAWT noise sources for targeted mitigation. Vertical axis wind turbines occupy a unique and increasingly important niche in the global renewable energy landscape. Their potential for reliable, quiet, omnidirectional energy harvesting in urban, decentralized, and offshore settings is clear. What remains to be established through rigorous, multi-disciplinary research is the quantitative realization of this potential in the complex, variable, and demanding environment of real world deployment.
