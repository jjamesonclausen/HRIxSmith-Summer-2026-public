## Abstract

Wind power is known as a promising renewable energy source without any environmental pollution. Increasing efficiency of wind turbines plays a vital role in wind energy applications, approving their high efficiency and cost-effectiveness. Variable pitch angle approaches are one of the best strategies for improving the performance and self-start ability of Darrieus turbines and delaying the start of dynamic stall phenomena. However, although this method has been successfully used on several occasions, it has not yet been investigated for various complicated variable pitch functions in different amplitudes. Therefore, the present study analyzed the relationship between the angle of attack and self-starting performance of H-type vertical axis wind turbine. Four pitch functions (cases 1, 2, 3 and Sinusoidal pitch) with four amplitudes; 0°, 3°, 10°, and 20° were selected, and two-dimensional CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) modeling was developed to predict flow behavior around the turbine blades. The results showed that the best variable pitch function could reduce the blade angle of attack in the upstream phase while increases the angle of attack in the downstream stage. In all cases, the amplitude of 3° has an excellent performance compared to other amplitudes. Also, compared to other pitch angles, Case 1 could reduce the time required for starting and with an increase of 34% in power, so it is strongly recommended for implementing in conventional turbines. Simulations also indicated that in azimuth angles between 90° and 100°, and between 45° and 60° turbine has the best and worst self-starting performance, respectively.

## 1. Introduction

Considering the global desire for reducing greenhouse gas emissions and providing sustainable energies that address energy consumption rising for energy types; many efforts are being made to develop renewable energies. The wind is one of the most reliable types of renewable energy. Despite fossil fuel power station, which produces a large amount of greenhouse gas, the wind turbines can generate electricity without releasing pollutants. The energy produced by wind farms depends on the strength of the wind at production time. It was reported in 2016 that wind farms in throughout England produced more electricity than coal power plants and produced more energy than solar panels. It is not surprising, therefore, that overall capacity of all wind turbines installed worldwide by the end of 2018 reached 597 Gigawatt. Thus, developing research and studies on improving the performance of wind turbines is a great way to get rid of worries about global warming, and contribute to the development the sustainable energy. Based on the direction of the rotation axis, wind turbines are divided into two Horizontal Axis Wind Turbines (HAWT) and Vertical Wind Axis Turbines (VAWTs).

HAWTs have the higher efficiency in generating electricity compared to VAWTs; however, some drawbacks such as requiring yaw mechanism, creating too much noise, and high costs for installing a generator at high altitudes draw tendencies toward VAWTs. On the other hand, VAWTs can get the wind from every direction. Therefore, a new mechanism is not required to adjust blades in wind direction. VAWTs can be categorized into two types, Darrieus and Savonius wind turbines.

## 2. Methodology

The simulation of the numerical method is conducted by using a 2D incompressible viscous Navier-Stokes equations. The governing equations are:

Continuity equation:
`V: V! = 0`

Momentum equations:
`X - Momentum` and `Y - Momentum` are written in the paper as the 2D viscous terms with pressure, gravity, and viscosity contributions.

Where `t` is time, `u` is velocity in the x-direction, `v` is velocity in the y-direction, `V!` is the velocity vector, `gx` is acceleration owing to gravity in the x-direction, `gy` is acceleration owing to gravity in the y-direction, `r` is density, `P` is pressure, and `m` is dynamic viscosity, and `v` is kinematic viscosity. In order to simulate the turbulence model, due to a right prediction of turbulence flow in adverse pressure gradients and separation flow, the SST `k-u` turbulence model is used to accurately predict the dynamic stall.

One of the most critical parameters in turbine simulations is TSR, which was described as follows:
`l = Ru / V∞`

The other parameter used in this study is the angle of attack (`a`), which is described as:
`a = tan^-1( sin q / (l + cos q) )`

Torque and power coefficient (`Cm` and `Cp`) are defined as follows:
`Cm = T / (1/2 rAsrV∞^2)`
`Cp = P / (1/2 rAsV∞^3)`

Equation (8) shows the relation between torque and power coefficient:
`Cp = Cm × l`

## 2.1. Computational domain and boundary conditions

In the 2D numerical simulation of a Darrieus wind turbine, the size of the computational domain should be selected such a way to minimize the blockage effects. As depicted in Fig. 2, the computational domain is a rectangular domain with 40.8R length and 27.2R width. The turbine was located in 13.6R from the inlet and lateral boundaries. Velocity inlet and pressure outlet were considered for left and right boundaries, respectively. Additionally, the symmetry boundary conditions were supposed on the lateral sides of the computational domain. Moreover, wall boundary conditions were chosen for the surface of the blades. The computational domain was divided into three different zones including a stationary zone, rotating zone, and blade zones. Besides, the interface boundary condition was assumed around the blades domain.

## 3. Results and discussions

### 3.1 Introducing pitch functions

In this section, harmonic pitch function will be introduced, after that CFD simulations will be conducted, and the performance of the turbine will be compared. Some researches showed that the pitch angle has a significant effect on the performance of Darrieus wind turbines. There is a relationship between the angle of attack and pitch angle (`b`) as Equation (9).

`aNEW = aold - b(q)`

In this equation, `b(q)` is pitch angle function which changes as a function of azimuth angle, `aold` is blade angle of attack before applying pitch function, and `aNEW` is blade angle of attack after applying pitch angle. According to this equation, by increasing pitch angle `b`, angle of attack `a` will decrease, and the blade can generate lift and torque even in low TSRs. To investigate the effect of pitch angle on the performance of the turbine, three pitch functions (Cases 1, 2 and 3) were selected, and simulation for each pitch function was performed for TSRs between 1 and 3.5. These functions are based on equation (10). In this formula, `A` is the pitch amplitude.

The difference between these functions is in azimuth angles, where the maximum angle of attack occurs. Similar to the sinusoidal pitch angle function; the amplitudes of 3°, 10°, and 20° were selected. For each pitch functions, the UDF code was programmed in C++ language, and the user's intention changes for rotation of the blades applied through this code. The new angle of attack was obtained from the summation of the old angle of attack and pitch angle. In UDF, equation (10) was applied, and with changing `A` and `B` parameters, different pitch functions were simulated.

### 3.2 Comparison of pitch functions

Case 1 pitch function is similar to the sinusoidal pitch function, but the azimuth angle related to maximum pitch was delayed in this case. In this function, the maximum pitch angle occurs at `q = 140°` in the upstream, and `q = 220°` in the downstream phase. On the other hand, in this function case 2, the maximum pitch function occurs at `q = 90°` in the upstream and `q = 270°` in the downstream. Regarding the pitch function case 3, the maximum pitch occurs at azimuth angle `q = 30°` in the upstream and `q = 330°` in the downstream phase.

Fig. 3 shows the initial angle of attack, the new angle of attack for all pitch functions with an amplitude of `A = 3°`, and at `l = 2`.

In Fig. 4, the curve of power coefficient versus TSR for the variable pitch functions (case 1, 2, and 3) is shown. For case 1 with amplitude of 3°, the performance of the turbine for all TSRs has been improved. However, for the amplitudes of 10° and 20°, the performance of the turbine after `l = 2.4` and `2.7` has been deteriorated, respectively. By increasing the amplitude of pitch function, the optimum TSR has been transmitted to lower TSRs, and if the amplitude increased, the optimum TSR would obtain earlier.

In Fig. 5, a comparison between the performance of turbine with variable pitch (case 1, 2 and 3), and the sinusoidal pitch function with amplitudes of 0°, 3°, 10°, and 20° has been represented.

The maximum power coefficient of the turbine is observed in the amplitude of 3°, and the power obtained from the variable pitch function for case 1 is more than the other cases, even sinusoidal functions. Also, compared to the fixed-pitch case, an increase of 34% in the power coefficient is observed for case 1. Another point that can be seen is for the amplitude of 20°, the power reduction is observed for all cases, and instead of increasing the power production, the efficiency of the turbine has been deteriorated.

### 3.3 Self-starting analysis

Darrieus wind turbines suffer from an inherent inability to start, and they often rely on engines to get started. The reason for the occurrence of this phenomenon can be interpreted by investigating the variation of the torque coefficient versus angle of attack. Self-starting is usually defined as the smooth movement of the turbine, while it can generate power simultaneously. In Fig. 7, the starting time of the Darrieus wind turbine versus azimuth angle (0°-120°) is shown. The results are shown for freestream velocity of 9 m/s and four cases, 1, 2, 3, and sinusoidal pitch angles at TSR of 1.5. At this TSR, the present wind turbine is located in the dead-band region and turbine cannot start itself.

Fig. 7 demonstrates that the turbine can self-start using different variable pitch methods and turbine. Furthermore, the turbine requires more time to start rotation at azimuth angles between 45° and 60°, while it has better starting performance at azimuth angles of 90°-100°. Therefore, when the first blade enters in azimuth angles between 90° and 100°, it has an excellent ability to start, and the imperfect self-starting capability is related to azimuth angles between 45° and 60°. The result also shows that compared to other pitch angles, Case 1 can reduce the time required for starting.

In Fig. 8, the time that the turbine needs to reach the different TSRs is presented. For variable pitch cases, pitch amplitude was adjusted `A = 3°`. As can be seen in the figure, the use of the pitch angle case 1 improves power efficiency. Also, in addition to its poor performance, the fixed-pitch turbine cannot reach higher TSRs. Therefore, it was concluded that the variable-pitch turbine using pitch function case 1 and `A = 3°` generates more power compared to other cases, as well, it improves the performance of self-starting.

### 3.4 Flow behavior around the H-type rotor

Also, the result indicates that the turbine with a fixed pitch angle or variable pitch case 3 are in the dead band area, and the turbine is unable to extend its working operation to higher TSRs. Fig. 9 shows static torque coefficient variation versus azimuthal angle for different variable pitch cases. This figure demonstrates that all variable pitch method cause a smooth, favorable torque distribution compared to fixed pitch case which aids the rotor to accelerate to the rated RPM. Comparing the torque coefficient values, variable pitch case 1 accomplishes more static torque than other cases. Therefore better starting and low wind speed performance are expected for the variable pitch turbine than the conventional configurations.

The flow visualization can help to understand the aerodynamics of flow around the rotor before and after using variable pitch control. Fig. 10 shows the vortices contour for cases 1, 2, and 3 for the fixed pitch blade at `l = 1` and `q = 160°`. The Analysis of flow around the rotor at this TSR helps to understand how an optimal pitch angle can improve the self-starting performance at low TSRs. As suggested by Amet et al. (2009), the behavior of the flow around the blade is influenced by the dynamic stall at low TSRs. For this reason, in this section, TSR of 1 was selected to analyze flow around the blade.

As shown in Fig. 10, concerning the streamlining pattern at `q = 160°`, the blade with a fixed pitch angle was entirely affected by the dynamic stall. From `q = 30°`, the vortices grew gradually and detached from the leading edge in the clockwise direction and trailing edge in the counter-clockwise direction. Also, as shown from vortex contours, compared to the zero pitch angles, smaller vortices were released for rest of variable pitch angles. This justifies that the optimum pitch control can save the blades from the dynamic stall.

In Figs. 11 and 12, the turbulent kinetic energy around the rotor is presented for both fixed and variable pitch angles (case1) with the amplitude of 10° and at TSR of 1.5. These contours are shown for four azimuth angles of 30°, 60°, 90°, and 120°. As can be seen, a turbine with variable pitch angle produces less wake than that of fixed-pitch angle. This observation implies that in a fixed pitch turbine, wind energy turns to vortices instead of converting wind energy into mechanical energy of the turbine rotation and it causes energy losses.

## 4. Conclusions

Wind energy is a renewable source of energy that plays an essential role in producing clean energy in the world. This paper attempts to help the development of wind turbines by introducing and optimizing an approach which increases the efficiency of Darrieus wind turbines.

In this study, the aerodynamic performance of the fixed and variable pitch turbine by using four different pitch functions (Case 1, 2, 3, and sinusoidal functions) was investigated using CFD simulations. To predict flow behavior around the wind turbine, CFD method was used very precisely. In Darrieus wind turbines, the angle of attack changed by variation of the TSR. Accordingly, at low TSRs, the angle of attack exceeded the dynamic stall angle, so the separation of the flow happens. Separating the flow around the blade decreased lift force at the low TSRs due to large amount of angle of attack, the power generated by the turbine reduced considerably.

Compared to other amplitudes, the amplitude of 3° had the best performance. Not only this amplitude did have a higher power coefficient at all TSRs but also did improve the self-starting capability compared to the fixed pitch angle in all cases. The numerical simulation revealed that Darrieus turbine in this function at TSR of 2 produces about 13% more power rather than sinusoidal pitch function. The reason behind this difference in power production is related to the angle of attack in azimuth angles between 0° and 25° in the sinusoidal function, where torque production has been reduced.

Furthermore, the pitch function of case 3 had the worst performance compared to other pitch functions; however, it slightly improved the turbine performance in comparison with the fixed-pitch turbine. Therefore, the case1 at the amplitude of 3° could be selected as a beneficial variable pitch angle function.

As part of future work, since simplifications are included in the two-dimensional analysis, it is recommended to perform three-dimensional simulations if there are software possibilities to improve the accuracy of the results. Additionally, the future works should address whether the blade pitch angle is just dependent on blading azimuth angle or other comprehensive control methods should be investigated.
