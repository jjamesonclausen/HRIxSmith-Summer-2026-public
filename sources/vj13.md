A B S T R A C T
The Savonius wind turbine cluster installation significantly impacts its power output in real wind fields. This
study aims to investigate the effect of wind conditions on the power output of a Savonius wind turbine cluster
and obtain the optimal installation orientation of the cluster in three selected sites. Two-dimension simulations
are conducted to evaluate the cluster performance, and a calculation method based on power curves is proposed
to evaluate its annual power output. Results showed that the wake effect induced by wind direction is the pri­
mary factor leading to the performance decrease of wind turbines. Optimal installation orientations of the cluster
at the three selected sites were obtained using the proposed method, and their corresponding power outputs are
1.25, 1.27, and 1.17 times that of the isolated turbine. The wind direction with high wind energy exerts greater
effects than that with high wind frequency in determining the optimal installation orientation. The actual wind
resource characteristics should be comprehensively considered in determining the optimal installation orienta­
tion in practice. This study provides guidance to the cluster installation in real wind conditions and contributes to
the advancement of urban wind energy solutions.
1. Introduction
Renewable energy utilization has rapidly increased worldwide
because of energy shortages and air pollution (Lydia et al., 2014). Wind
energy is a kind of widely developed clean energy with the potential to
reduce fuel consumption and CO2 level in the atmosphere (Bruno et al.,
2016; Chen et al., 2019a), which has been one of the most important
forms of renewable energies (Chen et al., 2019b; Saad et al., 2020). Wind
turbines are common devices for converting wind energy into mechan­
ical energy and then to electricity, which are divided into two categories
based on their rotational axis: horizontal axis wind turbine (HAWT) and
vertical axis wind turbine (VAWT). The large-scale HAWTs can harvest
wind energy efficiently in onshore and offshore wind turbine farms
(Wang et al., 2022b, 2023). However, they are unsuitable for areas with
high turbulence and low speed, such as urban environments. In addition,
the turbines in a wind farm experience the inter-farm wake effect, which
reduces the downstream turbine performance (Wang et al., 2023). By
contrast, VAWTs can perform well under harsh wind conditions (Chong
et al., 2017). Savonius vertical axis wind turbine (SVAWT) shows high
potential as a distributed micro off-grid power generator applied in the
urban area because of its independence from wind direction, high
start-up characteristic, and low maintenance cost (Noman et al., 2022;
Tian et al., 2022; Wilberforce et al., 2023).
However, SVAWT presents a major drawback of a lower power co­
efficient (CP) than HAVT and Darrieus VAWT (Hesami et al., 2022;
Zhang et al., 2023). Hence, SVAWT has been numerically and experi­
mentally investigated to improve its power coefficient. The previous
research on the performance improvement of SVAWTs can be catego­
rized by modifying the turbine structure (Type 1), adding flow
enhancement devices (Type 2), and applying SVAWT clusters (Type 3).
The partially collected results of the three main types are listed in
Table 1. The first type of research focuses on improving the power
output of a single SVAWT through structure optimization. However,
achieving a high CP of 0.3 in this manner is challenging because the
power generation of SVAWT relies heavily on drag force. Optimizing the
structure alone provides limited power gains. In the second type of
research, additional devices are incorporated into the SVAWT system to
improve performance. However, this introduces increased structural
complexity and often disrupts the flow direction independence of
SVAWTs. The additional devices enhance thrust on advancing blades
and reduce drag on retreating blades, exacerbating fluctuations in tur­
bine torque. These challenges highlight the limitations of solely focusing

on the power improvement of a single SVAWT. Therefore, alternative

being explored to overcome these limitations. (Nag and Sarkar, 2021).
A positive coupling effect can significantly improve the turbine
power outputs when several SVAWTs are properly arranged. The flow
mechanism of the positive interaction in SVAWTs can be attributed to
the Magnus effect and periodic coupling of local flow (Shigetomi et al.,
2011). Shaheen et al. (2015) developed an efficient triangular cluster
consisting of one downstream and two upstream turbines and achieved
an improvement of 34% in the average power coefficient (Cp) compared
with that of the isolated turbine. El-Baz et al. (2016) investigated the
performance of a specific SVAWT cluster layout and obtained the
maximum Cp by changing the rotational directions of three turbines.
Zhang et al. (2017) used the Kriging method and particle swarm opti­
mization to maximize the power output of the SVAWT cluster with a
power improvement of 22.9%. Zheng et al. (2019) used a genetic al­
gorithm to optimize the configuration of a three-turbine cluster and
achieved a higher Cp of 36.8% compared with that of the isolated tur­
bine. Belabes et al. (2016) investigated the effect of additional obstacles
on the liner SVAWT farms with 11 turbines, and the overall power
output of this farm was increased by 12% compared to that of the farm
without additional obstacles. These studies mainly focused on evalu­
ating the performance of SVAWT clusters or arrays under fixed wind
directions. However, wind directions are variable in real wind fields, so
a cluster optimized for one specific wind direction may experience a
decline in performance when exposed to different wind directions.
Therefore, a more comprehensive understanding of the performance of
SVAWT clusters and their inter-farm wake effects under real wind
conditions is necessary to optimize strategies for the planning, con­
struction, and operation of these clusters (Wu et al., 2022; Wang et al.,
2022a).
The wind direction and wake effect should be considered in installing
the SVAWT cluster in a real wind field. The wake effect occurs when
downstream turbines are exposed to the wake produced by upstream
turbines, leading to decreased power output (Patel et al., 2017). For
instance, Mereu et al. (2017) analyzed the effect of wind direction on
liner SVAWT farms and demonstrated that the optimal Cp is achieved
when the liner farm is perpendicular to the wind direction and Cp de­
creases by approximately 20% at a wind angle of 45◦. However,
avoiding the wake effect in a SVAWT cluster is impossible because a
close distance (less than 2D) between two SVAWTs is needed to achieve
a positive interaction, while the wake dispersion distance is usually 15D
(Zuo et al., 2016). There is limited research on SVAWT cluster perfor­
mance and optimal installation in real wind conditions. To address this
gap, it is crucial to consider the dynamic nature of wind directions and
comprehensively understand how it impacts the performance and
arrangement of the turbines within the cluster. Additionally, the lower
speed of incoming flow for downstream turbines in the wake region
compared to upstream turbines results in different optimal rotational
speeds for downstream turbines. However, in a cluster, the turbine
rotational speeds are typically set to the same value (fixed speed
method). Introducing a variable speed method is necessary to evaluate
the performance of the SVAWT cluster and allow SVAWTs in the cluster
to operate at their optimal power points.
This study aims to investigate the impact of wind conditions and
wake effects on the performance of a three-turbine Savonius cluster and
develop a calculation method to evaluate the annual power output of the
cluster in real wind fields. The study introduces two innovations: i)
examining the impact of wind conditions and wake effects on the power
output of the SVAWT cluster and ii) developing a calculation method for
determining the annual average power output and assessing the effect of
installation orientation. The remainder of this paper is structured as
follows: Section 2 introduces the SVAWT cluster geometry and numer­
ical method. Section 3 presents a variable speed method and investigates
the impact of wind conditions and wake effects on cluster performance.
Finally, Section 4 includes the power curve modeling, calculation flow
chart, and suggestions for cluster installation. The results obtained
through this study will contribute to the progress of urban wind energy
solutions.

The mesh and time step independence tests were conducted as an
essential process for the numerical simulations. A mesh test is carried
out using five kinds of mesh distribution to ensure the grid independence
of the computational domain with an isolated turbine. The corre­

the CP demonstrates a gradual decline followed by stabilization, reach­

results. The CP difference between mesh levels 3 and 5 is nearly 0.7%.
Therefore, mesh level 3 with 130,000 elements is chosen in this work,
and the same mesh distribution is used for the computational domain of
the three-turbine cluster. Finally, the total number of cluster mesh ele­

Table 3 is investigated, and the time step of 0.001 s is used in the

2.3. Numerical validation
A regular periodic torque fluctuation of the SVAWT was observed
after a few cycles, as shown in Fig. 5. Hence, the data of the last ten
cycles were selected for performance evaluation. The 2-D numerical
simulation results are validated by comparing them with the experi­
mental data (Sheldahl et al., 1978) under a wind velocity of 7 m/s. As
shown in Fig. 6, the 2-D simulation with low TSR (below 0.9) presents a
satisfactory fit with experimental data. The simulated optimal TSR (1.0)
is larger than the experimental results (0.9), and the simulation some­
what overestimates CP values at large TSR. The deviations are mainly
attributed to the incapability of accurately capturing the flow loss near
the endplate and vertical flow mixing in the 2-D simulations. Despite
these limitations, the maximum discrepancy between numerical and
experimental results remains within 5%, indicating that the numerical
method meets the accuracy requirements.

3.3. Flow analysis of the cluster in eight wind directions
The eight wind direction scenarios can be classified into two cate­
gories: those with no mutual interference between the turbine wakes
within the array (N, S, NE, and NW) and those with wake interference of
adjacent turbines (SW, SE, E, and W). Fig. 10 presents four flow fields for
N, S, NE, and NW wind directions. In these wind directions, there are two
noticeable high-speed flow regions between the turbines, which can
improve the turbine performance when the advancing blades of the
turbines are positioned in these regions. The advancing blades of T1 and
T3 of the cluster are all in the high-speed flow regions in N, NW, and NE
wind directions, resulting in a better performance than that in the S wind
direction.
Wake effect attributed to adverse wind direction markedly decreases
the power output of downstream turbines and is a crucial issue for wind
farm layout optimization (Guo et al., 2021). The turbines in the SE and
SW wind directions exhibit a minor decline in performance due to wake
interference. Wake interference can affect the upstream (T2 in Fig. 11a)
or downstream turbine (T3 in Fig. 11b), depending on the wind direc­
tion and the turbine arrangement. When the turbine is entirely located in
the wake region, it may even fail to generate power, such as in the cases
of the E and W wind conditions in Fig. 11c and d. For instance, the ve­
locity magnitude around T1 in the E wind direction is significantly lower
than that around T2 and T3 due to the blocking effect and energy con­
sumption caused by the upstream turbines. Another reason for the
performance difference between T1 and T2 lies in the adverse blade
position of T1 determined by its rotational direction. The returning
blade side of T1 in the periodic high-speed zone faces substantial
resistance from the high-speed flow, whereas the advancing blade side
of T2 is in the periodic high-speed zone, which benefits power output.
Under these conditions, the power output ratio F is very small (less than
0.1), and the rotational speed is set to 0 rad/s, as mentioned in Section
3.1.
As a result, the turbine wake significantly affects the power output of
the SVAWT cluster. Additionally, the wakes exhibit heightened turbu­
lence intensity, which can result in irregular torque fluctuation that may
impact the lifespan of the wind turbines. Therefore, reasonably
installing turbine clusters considering real wind direction characteristics
to minimize wake effects can significantly enhance power output and
lifespan.

4.2. Power output calculation
Installation orientation is crucial for the cluster deployed in a real
wind field, given that the wind direction will significantly affect the
performance of the SVAWT cluster. The installation orientation of the
cluster is defined in Fig. 12. The change of installation orientation means
adjusting the entire cluster to a direction without changing its layout.
The arrow line perpendicular to the center line of T1 and T3 represents
the installation orientation of the cluster. The calculation flow chart of
annual power output is given in Fig. 13. The power curves at eight wind
directions will change with the installation orientation. The annual
power output ratio at one orientation was calculated by dividing the
annual power output of the cluster by that of the isolated turbine. The
annual power output of the isolated turbine is a fixed value due to the
wind direction independence of Savonius turbine performance. How­
ever, the annual power output of the selected cluster is variable at
different installation orientations. Therefore, the optimal installation
orientation will be obtained by comparing the annual power output at
all installation orientations.
Wind frequency and energy in each direction are essential features in
regional wind resource assessment because they can be used to deter­
mine the optimal installation orientation of the cluster. Evident differ­
ences in wind characteristics can be observed in the wind direction and
energy rose diagrams of the three selected sites (Fig. 14). Site 1 (Houston
Intercontinental Airport) shows evident prevailing wind direction
(maximum wind frequency) and maximum wind energy in the same
direction. By contrast, a clear prevailing wind direction is observed in
the NE direction of Site 2 (New Orleans International Airport), although
the wind energy is obvious smaller than that in the SE and S directions.
Site 3 (Birmingham Airport) presents a more even frequency distribution
without a strong prevailing wind direction than the two other sites, but a
significantly larger energy density is observed in the W direction.

4.3. Effect of installation orientation
The maximum F values of the cluster (1.25, 1.27, and 1.16) at the
three selected sites are all higher than 1.0. This finding indicated that the
cluster could perform better than the isolated turbine in the real wind
field with multiple wind directions. The maximum F values are larger at
sites with concentrated wind energy distribution (Site 1 and 2) than that
at Site 3. Because the optimal installation orientation at Site 3 is in the W
direction, the wind in the N direction (perpendicular to the installation
orientation W) will decrease the cluster power output. The minimum F
values (0.816, 0.87, and 0.895) in three sites are all larger than that
simulated when the installation orientation of the cluster is N direction
(almost 0.4), which can be explained by considering the influence of
eight wind directions rather than a single direction. In addition, the
relative differences between the maximum and minimum values of F
were 52%, 46%, and 26% for Sites 1, 2, and 3, respectively, demon­
strating that the cluster installation orientation plays an important role
in the power output. It can be concluded that the more concentrated the
distribution of wind energy, the greater the optimal power output.
Another interesting finding is that the optimal installation orienta­
tion does not always coincide with the wind direction with the
maximum wind energy or maximum wind frequency. For instance, the
wind direction with the maximum wind frequency for Site 2 is the NE
direction, which exhibits extreme adverse orientation for the cluster
installation with the minimum F value. By comparison, the optimal
installation orientation (SE) is in the direction with high wind energy, as
shown in the wind energy rose (Fig. 14b). The wind direction with high
wind energy presents greater effects than the wind direction with high
wind frequency when determining the optimal installation orientation.

SVAWT clusters are ideal for urban energy solutions by harnessing

5. Conclusions and limitations
5.1. Conclusions
Two-dimensional transient CFD simulations were carried out in the
present study to calculate the turbine performance under different wind
conditions by embedding the variable rotational speed control strategy
through a UDF code. Thirty-two cases under eight wind directions and
four wind speeds were simulated to investigate their effect on the power
output of the cluster. The operations and flow fields of downstream
turbines in the wake region were investigated. A power curve model was
established based on the CFD results to evaluate the annual power
output of the cluster in three selected sites. The following conclusions
are drawn.
1. The influence of wind direction on cluster performance is more sig­
nificant than that of wind speed. Variations in cluster performance
resulting from changes in wind direction can exhibit disparities of up
to 350%. Wind directions perpendicular to the installation orienta­
tion represent extremely adverse conditions, while wind from ±45◦
angles relative to the installation orientation proves to be efficiently
harnessed for energy generation. The flow field analysis reveals that
the cluster’s power output is related to the wind direction and tur­
bine arrangement.
2. A new calculation flow chart based on power curves was established
to assess the average power output for various cluster installation
orientations. Optimal installation orientations have been determined
for the three selected sites, and the corresponding power improve­
ments are 25%, 27%, and 16%, respectively. Notably, wind di­
rections with high wind energy demonstrate more pronounced
impacts on determining the optimal installation orientation than
those with high wind frequency.
Furthermore, the proposed calculation method of annual power
output offers valuable guidance for installing small-scale SVAWT clus­
ters. It can be extended for power output evaluation of Darrieus turbine

5.2. Limitations
It is important to address the limitations of the current study and
consider them in future research.
1. The proposed annual power output calculation method was based on
hourly-averaged wind data without considering the transient change
in wind conditions. The stability process of SVAWTs is relatively
rapid, making the power output calculated through this method
comparatively accurate. Conducting CFD simulations over extended
timeframes is necessary to mitigate assessment inaccuracies in future
work.
2. The 2-D simulations do not account for the flow losses near the end
plate and vertical mixing flow, leading to discrepancies with
experimental results. Future efforts will encompass more precise
calculations to improve accuracy.
