## Abstract

Vertical axis wind turbines (VAWTs) have distinctive attributes, for example, exceptional performance in built environments and high packing density in wind farms. This study reviews recent research in the wake aerodynamics of H-rotor VAWTs. The general mathematical expressions for the analysis of the rotor aerodynamics of VAWTs are presented, followed by a classic aerodynamic model. Velocity deficit and turbulence increase are common features of wind turbine wakes, and the deep dynamic stall of operational VAWTs further complicates their wakes. This study systematically compiles the investigations of the wake flow fields and vortical structures of VAWTs. In these investigations, a wide range of research methods have been used, for instance, wind tunnel testing, particle image velocimetry (PIV) tests, field tests, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations, and theoretical development. The results of these studies suggest that the wakes of VAWTs are characterized by distinctive features, such as strong asymmetry and counter-rotating vortical motion. Based on these characteristics, researchers have developed various wake models. The proposed wake models can be used to design standalone VAWTs or layouts for multiple VAWTs. Finally, this paper provides discussions and suggestions pertaining to the investigations of the wakes of VAWTs.

## 1. Introduction

In recognition of the precipitous depletion of fossil fuels and the alarming deterioration of the global climate, nations worldwide are strongly supporting the development of renewable energy sources such as wind and solar energy. Among renewable energy sources, wind energy, which is clean and environmentally friendly, is considered the most cost-effective source [1e4]. The installed capacity of wind power worldwide has increased remarkably in the past decade [5e9]. For many centuries, ingeniously designed wind turbines have harnessed wind energy. Modern wind turbines are categorized into two main groups depending on the orientation of rotational axis: horizontal axis wind turbines (HAWTs) and vertical axis wind turbines (VAWTs) [10]. HAWTs have dominated the wind power industry for several decades. However, HAWTs have some inherent problems, such as size ceiling due to gravity-induced reversing stress at blade roots [11] and deficiencies under turbulent flows [12,13]. Recent research suggests that VAWTs have great potential for use in urban installations [14e16] and exhibit fast wake recovery, which means high packing densities [17e19]. This has led to a resurgence of interest in VAWTs [20,21]. Although VAWTs have simple structural configurations, analyzing their aerodynamics has been challenging the wind energy community for decades [22,23,130]. The primary difficulty lies in the fact that the angle of attack (AOA, a) of VAWTs experiences constant changes within each revolution, unlike that of HAWTs [24]. The rapidly changing AOA induces strong vortex shedding from the leading and trailing edges of blades, thus inducing highly unsteady lift fluctuations and fatigue loads on blades [25e27]. This phenomenon is widely known as dynamic stall characterized by vortex formation and its release to the wake, and, to a great extent, it defines the aerodynamics and wake of VAWTs. Dynamic stall theories were originally developed for analyzing helicopter rotors [28e30], and dynamic stall of VAWTs has been studied since the early 1980s. Dynamic stall is affected by various parameters such as Reynolds number [31], turbulence intensity [32,33], blade geometry [27,34], rotation rates [35], and reduced frequency [23,36].

Noll and Ham [37] developed a dynamic stall theory by using an empirical representation of the dynamic stall angle to analyze loads on and dynamic responses of rotor blades. Their results indicated that the peak lift loads (CL z 3.0) and moments (CM z 0.65) acting on rotor blades during dynamic stall were around 3 and 5 times higher than the respective static values (CL z 0.9, CM z 0.13). Laneville and Vittecoq [38] conducted an experimental investigation of the dynamic stall of a VAWT at different blade speed ratios (BSRs, l = VB/U with VB for blade speed and U for freestream velocity). They concluded that helicopter rotor aerodynamics could be applied to the dynamic stall behaviors of VAWT blades. Fujisawa and Shibuya [39] combined flow visualizations and particle image velocimetry (PIV) tests to investigate dynamic stall of VAWT blades. They observed two pairs of counter-rotating vortices evolving toward the wake. To further understand the BSR effect on dynamic stall, the process of the roll-up, release and interaction with blades passing downwind passage was visualized using PIV tests [22,130]. In recent years, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations have emerged as a suitable technique to examine the complex dynamic stall features (Ferreira et al., 2007; [40,131]. Almohammadi et al. [25] carried out two-dimensional (2-D) CFD simulations using two turbulence models to analyze dynamic stall of a VAWT. The transition shear stress transport (SST) model [41] outperformed the SST keu model [42] in predicting the flow transition. Here the transition means the laminar-to-turbulent transition, the onset of which is determined by the intermittency and momentum thickness. The transition significantly affected the turbine performance by up to 20%. Rosado Hau et al. [23] conducted CFD simulations to analyze the influence of VAWT parameters on the moment of stall inception. The stall-onset angle was observed to increase linearly with the non-dimensional pitch rate. Huang et al. [33] assessed the effect of freestream turbulence on dynamic stall using large eddy simulation (LES) and revealed that freestream of 5% turbulence level improved the aerodynamic performance of blades. Research on VAWTs can be classified into two major categories [43]: (a) rotor aerodynamics analysis and (b) wake aerodynamics analysis. In rotor aerodynamics analysis, velocities in the proximity of blades, forces on blades, and power coefficients of turbines are the most concerned parameters. The main objective of rotor aerodynamics analysis is to improve the aerodynamic performance of blades and, thereby, increase the power generated by individual VAWTs. Rotor aerodynamics has been analyzed extensively over the past four decades [44,45]. The pioneering work dates back to the 1970s when Templin [46] derived an analytical streamtube model. Based on the streamtube model [47e49], proposed and developed a more advanced double multiple streamtube (DMS) model. Recently, Saber et al. [50] introduced a new relation to evaluate the equilibrium induced air velocity to improve the power prediction results using the classical DMS model. Moghimi and Motawej [51] developed a low-cost DMS model to assess parametric effects on the performance of VAWTs and found that large chord length contributed to good self-starting performance. Still, the DMS model tends to notably overestimate the power performance of VAWTs [52]. Compared with rotor aerodynamics, wake aerodynamics studies are in their nascency and have only started to attract researchers’ attention in recent years [53e55]. Wake analysis focuses on the development of flow fields and vortical structures downstream, as well as on the modeling of these behaviors. As the wake flows are characterized by large velocity deficits and high turbulence, scholars are working to reduce their mutual interference and maximize the total power output of multiple closely packed turbines. Note that the large velocity deficit means that the freestream velocity may encounter as large as 75% deductions in the wake [53]. As aerodynamic models can determine the mean flow fields but not the turbulent structures [24], experiments, numerical simulations and wake modeling have been used instead in the literature to investigate the wake aerodynamics of VAWTs. This study comprehensively reviews recent progress in the wake aerodynamics analysis of H-rotor VAWTs. Section 1 introduces the research background and the challenges associated with the analysis of VAWTs due to dynamic stall. The general classification of modern VAWTs based on their driven modes and geometrical forms is given in Section 2. Wake investigations have contributed substantially to the development and successful operation of modern wind farms. The literature on the wake aerodynamics of VAWTs is comprehensively examined in Section 3. The review concludes with Section 4, which presents a summary and recommendations for future work.

## 2. Classification of VAWTs

VAWTs are classified into two basic categories based on the forces driving the rotor blades: (a) drag-driven Savonius VAWTs and (b) lift-driven Darrieus VAWTs. In general, Savonius VAWTs are less efficient in harnessing wind energy than Darrieus VAWTs [56,57].

### 2.1. Savonius VAWTs

Savonius VAWTs were invented by and named after the Finnish engineer S.J. Savonius in 1929 [11,58]. Fig. 1 shows the schematic of a Savonius VAWT. As the concave sides of the cup-shaped blades catch the approaching wind, large drag forces are induced (see Fig. 2). In contrast, relatively small drag forces are generated when the oncoming flow blows onto the convex sides. Therefore, a driving torque is formed due to the differences in the drag forces acting on the blades, and this torque drives the blades to rotate around a vertical shaft. Because Savonius VAWTs are driven by drag forces, they cannot rotate faster than wind speed, and their rated blade speed ratios (BSRs) are less than 1.0. They transform the kinetic energy of wind into mechanical energy, generating electric

Savonius VAWTs are generally used for and have achieved great success in wind-speed-measurement applications rather than electric-power-generation applications [62,63].

### 2.2. Darrieus VAWTs

Darrieus VAWTs were invented by the French engineer G.J. Darrieus [64]. In lift-driven Darrieus VAWTs, oncoming flows are deflected onto the upper and lower surfaces of an airfoil blade as the flows approach the leading edge of the blade (see Fig. 3). Consequently, a lift force is produced on the blade due to the presence of pressure difference. This lift force overcomes the drag force and generates a tangential component. The tangential force, together with the blade radius, forms the driving torque that rotates the turbine to produce electric power. Modern Darrieus VAWTs can be classified into three basic categories: (a) Egg-beater VAWTs, (b) Helical VAWTs and (c) H-rotor VAWTs.

### 2.2.1. Egg-beater VAWT

Egg-beater Darrieus VAWTs containing two or more curved airfoil blades are also called curved-bladed VAWTs [63,65]. The airfoil blade is bent from the blade mid-span toward its two ends (see Fig. 4). This type of blade configuration helps to minimize the bending stress in the blades of egg-beater VAWTs and is beneficial for the structural design. The Eole of 3.8-MW is the largest eggbeater VAWT ever built, thanks to its favorable structural configuration [66]. As wind blows over the airfoil blades, aerodynamic lift is produced. The flow around an egg-beater VAWT is intrinsically erratic, leading to inherently unsteady loadings on blades [24,56]. In the 1980s, several prototypes of egg-beater VAWTs were developed in Canada and the US [10]. However, because of the unfavorable aerodynamic loadings, these turbines suffered from problems such as blade fatigue, cracks in tower foundation, and bottom bearing failure [10]. Moreover, the straight-bladed VAWT is more cost effective than the egg-beater VAWT [62].

### 2.2.2. H-rotor VAWT

An H-rotor VAWT equipped with two or more straight blades is also called a straight-bladed VAWT, giromill, or cycloturbine (see Fig. 5). The straight blades are connected to the central shaft by means of struts. This structural configuration simplifies the manufacture of H-rotor VAWTs relative to the egg-beater VAWTs. Researchers have pointed out that small-scale VAWTs accepting winds from all directions (360) are suitable for urban applications [67e69]. ### 2.2.3. Helical VAWT

Helical VAWTs developed by A.M [79,80]. are derivatives from the H-rotor VAWTs and aims mainly for hydraulic power. Helical VAWTs have inclined blades (see Fig. 6), and the inclined angle is named as the helical angle. Note that the helical angle is 90 for an H-rotor VAWT. Helical VAWTs with blades overlaping the entire circumference of the turbine are capable of reducing the torque ripples of H-rotor VAWTs [55]. The helical turbines are mainly used for and demonstrate effectiveness in harnessing hydraulic power [81e83]. The helical tidal turbines showed good starting characteristics while had poor performace in energy production compared to their H-rotor counterparts [84e86]. Castelli and Benini [87] reported a comparative study of the power performance of H-rotor VAWTs and helical VAWTs using CFD simulations. The power of the helical turbine dropped significantly at BSRs of 2.6e4.1 compared to its H-rotor counterpart. Moreover, the helical VAWT caused more flow distortion with affected regions of 4.2D (D for turbine diameter) than did by the H-rotor VAWT with affected regions of 2.5D [88]. Besides, the blades of helical VAWTs are complex and inevitably more expensive to manufacture than their H-rotor counterparts [89].

## 3. Wake aerodynamics of H-rotor VAWTs

### 3.1. Experimental analysis

Experimental measurements under controlled conditions are fundamental for the wake analysis of H-rotor VAWTs. There are three major categories of experimental methods for studying wake characteristics: (1) wind tunnel testing, (2) PIV testing and (3) field testing. In wind tunnel testing, cobra probes or hot-wire anemometers on a traversing system and force measurement system are commonly used. In PIV testing, the flow field is seeded with small particles and illuminated using a laser beam for visualization. Photos of the seeded particles are used to calculate the wind speed and direction. Field testing which reflects the real operating conditions functions as an important fact-checking method for wake analysis.

### 3.1.3. Field test

Compared with a great many of PIV tests and wind tunnel tests, there have been only a few field tests about the wake of VAWTs because of capital costs and technical difficulties. Dabiri conducted a series of field tests to assess the wind farm power density of arrays of VAWTs [17]. Over the testing period from June to September in 2010, the average wind speed and turbulence fluctuation were 7.8 m/s and 2.6 m/s, respectively. The turbine rotational speed and electrical power were measured and recorded at a sampling rate of 1 Hz. Each measurement was assigned a time stamp to synchronize with separately collected meteorological data. A meteorological tower measuring 10 m was erected 15D northwest of the nearest VAWT to avoid any flow interference. Furthermore, the wind flow differences at the meteorological tower and VAWTs were assumed non-significant by typical turbulence fluctuations of 30%e40%. The counter-rotating mechanism (CBR according to Ref. [94] helped to orient the airflow outwards. Hence, a third VAWT could be positioned in the wake of the counter-rotating VAWTs. Moreover, the vertical flux of kinetic energy from above toward the wake of VAWTs further facilitated their close spacing. To investigate the real wake properties, field measurements were performed by erecting masts with three-cup and ultrasonic anemometers (see Fig. 21) [99]. A turbine with a diameter of 2 m was installed. The reference wind speed was measured by averaging data from two ultrasonic anemometers on masts A and B. The velocity field in the wake was measured using five ultrasonic anemometers at 5 m high downstream of the wind turbine. Only the flow data from the prevailing wind direction of 270 ± 30 with a bin width of 2.5 were processed. ### 3.2.1. RANS modeling

In RANS modeling, the flow properties are written by applying Reynolds decomposition to have the time-averaged quantities and fluctuating terms. With the continuity and momentum equations applied, the RANS equations can be constructed [100,101]. In the literature, Different types of turbulence models have been proposed to resolve the unknown Reynolds stresses in the RANS equations [102,103]. Ferreira et al. conducted 2-D RANS CFD simulations of a singlebladed VAWT to analyze dynamic stall and vortical structures in the near wake [131]. Both the one-equation SpalarteAllmaras (SeA) model [103] and the two-equation Re-normalization group (RNG) keε model [104] were used for turbulence modeling. The moving sub-grid method was used to model the rotor movement. By comparing with PIV results, it was found that the RNG keε model outperformed the S-A model in estimating the average vortical distributions, for instance, the rolling up of the trailing-edge vortices. The study examined the effect of a single blade on the flow field rather than the collateral effects of all blades.

### 3.2.2. LES modeling

In LES models, large-scale eddies (anisotropic) are directly resolved with small-scale eddies (isotropic) modeled using subgrid scale (SGS) models. Unlike the time-averaging method in RANS models, LES performs spatial filtering of the Navier-Stokes equations with a filter width of D [106]. After implementing the filter operation, the flow properties can be decomposed into directly solved quantities and modeled quantities. As a result, the filtered Navier-Stokes equations can be constructed. In the literature, a series of SGS models have been developed to calculate these SGS stresses in the filtered Navier-Stokes equations [106e108]. Shamsoddin and Porte-Agel [109] were among the first scholars to perform LES studies of the wake of H-rotor VAWTs. The Smagorinsky model [106] and the modulated gradient model (MGM) [110] were adopted to model the SGS stresses. Instead of directly resolving the boundary layer flows by LES, the actuator sweptsurface model (ASSM) and the actuator line model (ALM) were developed to calculate the turbine-induced forces. In doing so, the computational cost was substantially reduced. In a comparison with experimental data, the ALM yielded superior wake predictions over the ASSM. The MGM was able to capture the mean and turbulent flow properties more accurately than the Smagorinsky model. The wake structures of an H-rotor VAWT were studied using a complete LES model including blade tips [111]. The wake characteristics were examined at a lower and higher BSRs of 1.35 and 2.21, respectively. The LES prediction results exhibited good qualitative agreement with PIV results. To identify the vortical structures surrounding the turbine, iso-surfaces of low pressures colored by the vorticity magnitude were produced (see Fig. 29). The results revealed that at the lower BSR, larger coherent structures were generated, and these structures exhibited higher asymmetry due to higher AOAs, which further induced a stronger dynamic stall. BSR was a critical parameter for determining wake characteristics, and further research was required to analyze this correlation. Peng et al. [43] comprehensively studied the VAWT wake using a full-scale LES model. The algebraic wall-modeled LES was used [112]. Quantitative comparisons between the LES-predicted and measured wake velocities showed good agreement. At higher BSRs, the occurrence of dynamic stall was suppressed, consistent with the findings of Posa et al. [111]. The causes for wake asymmetry were addressed. First, the deep dynamic stall induced by the advancing rotor caused strong vortex shedding along the windward. Second, the Magnus force helped transport the wake flows towards the windward. The vortex-ring structures, counterrotating vortical motions and wake asymmetry were regarded as the signature features of VAWT wakes. Hezaveh et al. [113] investigated the effects of turbine parameters on the wake development of an H-rotor VAWT by LES. Similar to the work of Shamsoddin and Porte-Agel [109]; ALM was used to capture the blade-to-turbine-scale flow dynamics, while LES was applied to resolve the turbine-to-farm-scale flow structures. The turbine parameters could alter the wake shapes and intensities. Also, the turbine aspect ratio was considered as the most important parameter in the design of cluster configurations. Once the aspect ratio was fixed, the optimal turbine solidity could be inferred. Posa [114] examined the wake interactions of three types of twin VAWTs using LES. The twin turbine configurations were similar to the twin units considered by Ref. [94]. The iso-surfaces of pressures colored by the vorticity magnitude were attained to visualize the vortical structures of three types of coupled VAWTs (see Fig. 30). Subfigures at the left-hand side showed the vorticity field of three coupled VAWTs at a BSR of 2.21 compared with that to the right-hand side at a BSR of 1.35. The computational results indicated that the blockage effect helped increase the momentum flux between twin turbines, even at downstream locations. The blockage effect, rather than mutual vortex suppression, was considered the primary reason for the improved power performance of twin units.

The collapse of wake shear layers induced by rotating blades promoted the wake recovery. Besides, turbulent kinetic energy downstream in the wake became augmented with increasing BSR. Strengthened turbulence would induce the destabilization of shear layers and hence the wake recovery.

### 3.2.3. DES modeling

The standard LES models encountered computational difficulties, particularly in near-wall regions. Spalart et al. [119] proposed a hybrid model that combined the best aspects of RANS and LES methods in one solution strategy. This hybrid technique is named as the detached eddy simulation (DES) approach where the RANS models are employed in boundary layers, while the LES treatment is applied to separated regions [120]. Villeneuve et al. [121] improved the efficiency and wake recovery of H-rotor VAWTs via installing detached end plates (DEPs) below and above blade tip ends (see Fig. 34). The delayed DES model [122] was used for analysis. Though the blade tip vortices were present for turbines with circular and semi-annular DEPs, the vortices were far less intense (see Fig. 34). As the tip vortices were alleviated and redirected towards mid spans, both the power efficiency and the wake recovery of turbines with DEPs were noticeably improved. Scholars extend the wake studies to floating VAWTs. Lei et al. [123] investigated the wake of floating H-rotor VAWTs with pitch and surge motions (see Fig. 35) by the improved delayed DES approach. The wake characteristics were examined under various pitching and surging amplitudes and periods. ‘Spindle’-type of wake structures were produced under the surge motion. The strength and core zone of the ‘Spindle’ structures moved downstream and weakened with the progress of the surge motion (see Fig. 36). The pitch motion significantly altered the wake profiles, which further escalated downwind.

### 3.3. Wake model development

The wake aerodynamics of H-rotor VAWTs are considerably more complex than their HAWT counterpart due to dynamic stall. Hence, further efforts are needed to develop wake models fit for VAWTs. In general, the wake region can be divided into the near wake up to approximately 2D downstream and the far wake region stretching farther away [97,98]. Studies on the near and far wakes aim to improve the performance of standalone and multiple VAWTs, respectively. Studies of the near wake focus primarily on rotor aerodynamics, including dynamic stall and tip vortices. In contrast, studies of the far wake focus on mutual interference of multiple turbines in wind farms.

### 3.3.1. Near-wake model

The blades traveling in the downstream half-revolution interact with the vortices shed during the upstream half-revolution, causing interference. ## 4. Conclusions

This paper reviewed the basic categories of VAWTs and the significant progress in studies of the wake aerodynamics of H-rotor VAWTs. The main purpose of wake studies, which are still in their nascency, is to reduce mutual interference, and thereby maximize the total power output of multiple VAWTs. In this review, the recent progress of wake characteristics of VAWTs is examined in terms of research methods, e.g., experiment analysis, numerical simulation, and theoretical development. The wake region can be divided into the near wake and far wake. The near wake spans approximately two rotor diameters downstream and is characterized by dynamic stall behaviors, e.g., vortex shedding, rolling up, detachment, reattachment, etc. The far wake is less influenced by Reynolds number and geometrical effects and demonstrates self-similarity, and is suitable for wake modeling. The wake demonstrates strong asymmetry in the horizontal direction due to the occurrence of deep dynamic stall at windward and the Magnus effect and shifts slightly downward in the vertical direction. The counter-rotating vortical structures constituted the most important mechanism for imparting momentum to the wake for recovery. Given the special vortical structures, efficient twin and triangular array units of VAWTs can be configured for potential order-of-magnitude enhancement of packing density. Apart from configured array units, far-wake models can be constructed based on either momentum or continuity conservation. To use the asymmetrical far-wake model for layout design, parameters from three aspects are required: (1) basic turbine parameters, e.g., turbine diameter, power curve, etc., and model factors, e.g., spreading rates, velocity deficit at turbine center, etc.; (2) wind statistics and wind farm dimensions; and (3) optimization algorithm. In general, the power output of VAWTs in the optimal layout can be over 10% higher than in the initial layout. The wake studies have been in their nascency, most of which were performed in low-turbulence smooth flows. In the future, significant research efforts will be required to examine the wake aerodynamics, especially in grid-turbulence and ABL flows, to reflect the real operating conditions of VAWTs.
