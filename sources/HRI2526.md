## **Glossary and Abbreviations** 

## **Glossary** 

- **Arbitrary Mesh Interface (AMI)** : A technique in computer simulations that allows two different 3D models to "talk" to each other at a boundary even if their mesh grids do not align. 

- **Capacity Factor (CF)** : A ratio that estimates how often within a timeframe the turbine is operating at its rated power conditions. 

- **Coefficient of Power (CP)** : A common measure of wind turbine efficiency defined as the ratio between the power delivered by the turbine (PT) and the total power available in the wind (PW). 

- **Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)** : The use of computers to simulate how liquids and gases flow, interact, and transfer heat. 

- **Cut-in Speed** : The wind speed at which the turbine begins spinning. 

- **Cut-out Speed** : The wind speed at which the turbine is shut down to prevent damage. 

- **Multiple Reference Frames (MRF)** : A simulation technique that acts like a "frozen" snapshot to model rotating parts without moving the computer model. 

- **Nominal Capacity (CN)** : The rated capacity of a battery at the beginning of its life. 

- **Rated Power** : The maximum power output of a turbine. 

- **Rated Speed** : The wind speed at which the maximum power output is reached. 

- **Reynolds Number** : A formula/value used to predict if fluid flow will be smooth (laminar) or chaotic (turbulent). 

- **Starting Torque** : The torque generated by the turbine before it begins rotating. 

- **Swept Area (S)** : The product of the height and diameter of the turbine. 

- **Tip Speed Ratio (TSR)** : The ratio between the tip speed to the turbine and the free stream velocity. 

- **Torque Coefficient/Moment Coefficient (CT)/(CM)** : A nondimensional measure of the torque produced by a turbine. 

- **Yaw System** : The mechanism that rotates the turbine's top housing to align the rotor blades directly into the wind.

## **3.1 HAWTs vs VAWTs** 

There are two broad categories of wind turbine: VAWTs and HAWTs (Fig. 1). VAWTs have an axis of rotation perpendicular to the direction of the wind. These differ from HAWTs, which have an axis of rotation parallel to the wind. 

Fig. 1. Horizontal axis wind turbine (HAWT) (left) and Vertical axis wind turbine (VAWT) (right). [5] 

VAWTs and HAWTs have unique strengths and weaknesses which make them better suited for different applications. HAWTs are designed to operate well in open areas with consistent wind conditions and are typically used in large wind farm applications in remote and offshore 

locations with access to clean and undisturbed air [6]. Their ideal aerodynamic efficiency is reported within the range of 40-55% under steady wind, however, their performance diminishes sharply in low or unsteady wind conditions [6]. To function well, their rotors need to be facing the wind flow, so they are usually equipped with a self-starter and a yaw system [6][7]. 

In contrast, VAWTs are omnidirectional, allowing them to take advantage of winds with changing flow directions without a direction control mechanism [7]. In addition, they have a lower “cut-in speed,” which is defined as the minimum wind speed at which the blades begin to rotate, allowing them to take advantage of slower winds [6]. However, VAWTs are a much less developed technology and struggle with low efficiencies and market penetration. Because of this, nearly all of the electricity generation in the U.S. from wind turbines comes from HAWTs. [8].

## **3.2 Relevant Parameters** 

There are many parameters related to the performance of VAWTs that we will be using throughout the rest of this report. In this section, we will define some of these key terms. 

A power curve, which plots power output with respect to wind speed, can be used to evaluate the performance of a wind turbine (Fig. 3). There are three key regions of a power curve, bounded by the cut-in speed, the rated-speed, and the cut-out speed. The cut-in speed is defined as the wind speed at which the turbine begins spinning. At wind speeds below the cut-in speed, the turbine will not be generating power. 

Fig 3. Generalized wind turbine power curve showing cut-in speed, rated speed, and rated power. [14] 

After the cut-in speed has been reached, as the wind speed increases, the turbine’s power output increases exponentially until the rated wind speed is reached. At this point, the power output levels out to the rated power. As the wind speed increases from the rated speed, the power output remains constant until the cut-out speed is reached, when the turbine will usually be shut down to prevent damage. The turbine produces the most power when the wind speed is between the rated and cut-out speed, however, in urban areas, the wind speeds are often much lower than the rated 

speed. Another key parameter related to the cut-in of a turbine, is the starting torque, which is defined as the torque generated by the turbine before it begins rotating. A turbine will be able to cut-in or self-start, if its starting torque exceeds the static friction in the bearings and power electronics [15]. If a turbine has low starting torque, that indicates that it will need an external power source to help start it. ## **3.3 Types of VAWTs** 

There are three main types of VAWTs: Darrieus turbines, Savonius turbines, and hybrid turbines. This section introduces each type of turbine, its advantages and disadvantages, and work that has been done to improve its performance. 

## **3.3.1 Darrieus Turbines** 

Darrieus turbines are lift-based turbines meaning they generate electricity using aerodynamic lift to turn the rotor. As air flows around the airfoil shaped blades, a high pressure zone is created on one side of the blade while a low pressure zone is created on the other, as seen in Fig. 4. The resulting pressure imbalance generates a lift force acting perpendicular to the wind direction relative to the blades, as shown in Fig. 5 [16]. 

Fig. 4. Pressure contour from Darrieus turbine CFD showing high pressure above the airfoil blade and low pressure below it causing rotating in the counterclockwise direction [17] 

Fig 5. Darrieus turbine working principal [18] 

There are three main types of Darrieus turbines, which are shown below in Fig. 6. These include the D-rotor, also referred to as the Eggbeater Darrieus, the H-rotor Darrieus, and the helical Darrieus. The three types of Darrieus rotors are all lift-based turbines, but their different shapes can lead to variation in self-starting abilities, torque consistency, and manufacturability. The standard H-rotor Darrieus has a uniform angle of attack, while the eggbeater and helical Darrieus rotors have varying angle of attacks, due to the curvature of the blades. 

Fig. 6. Different Types of Darrieus VAWTs [19] 

Once a Darrieus turbine has begun to rotate, it is able to rotate at speeds much higher than the free-stream wind velocity, resulting in a TSR greater than 1. This makes it particularly effective at generating electricity at higher wind speeds, where the lift-based mechanism can be fully utilized [20]. Darrieus turbines are more efficient than drag-based Savonius turbines and typically achieve efficiencies of 30% to 40%. Due to their consistent and smooth energy output, 

Darrieus turbines are useful in situations that require consistent energy output, like larger scale applications [21]. 

However, Darrieus turbines typically are not able to start on their own and depend on an external starting mechanism to initiate their rotation, which is a major drawback [20]. Much of the research into Darrieus turbines has focused on improving their self starting ability to address this shortcoming. A more detailed discussion of this work can be found in Appendix B. 

## **3.3.2 Savonius Turbines** 

Savonius turbines are drag based turbines, meaning the force responsible for their rotation is the drag force [22]. Drag is a force which acts in the opposite direction of the motion of any object moving with respect to the surrounding fluid. A classical two-bladed Savonius turbine consists of two semicircular surfaces on opposite sides of a vertical shaft, one convex and the other concave as shown in Fig. 7 [22][23]. As the wind comes into contact with the two opposite facing surfaces, lift and drag forces are exerted on them [24]. A greater drag force is exerted on the concave surface than the convex one, causing the rotation of the turbine [23]. Since Savonius turbines operate on drag, they cannot rotate faster than the wind speed. This means that the tip speed ratio (TSR) is always between 0 and 1 for Savonius turbines [22]. Fig. 8 shows two examples of Savonius turbines: one classical, and the other helical.

Savonius turbines have many advantages including their simple construction and installation, low cost, ability to operate at slow wind speeds, and their self-starting capacity [22][23][25]. The low operating speed of Savonius turbines also make them lower noise compared to other VAWTs. These qualities make them attractive for regions with high turbulence intensity and low wind speeds [23]. However, they also suffer from major drawbacks, the most significant being high negative torque generated during operation and the associated loss of efficiency. Negative torque is the resistive force that opposes the turbine’s rotation, caused by the drag force on the returning blade as the wind pushes on it. This negative torque forces the turbine to overcome an additional force to start or continue rotating [23]. 

To decrease negative torque and improve the CP of Savonius turbines, researchers have worked to optimize various parameters of the turbine including the number of stages, the number of blades (n), the overlap ratio (OR), the aspect ratio (AR), and the blade shape [25]. Another method researchers have focused on to decrease negative torque is through augmentation devices that direct windflow toward the convex blade and away from the concave blade. While these strategies have been effective at increasing the coefficient of power, they often increase the size and complexity of the turbine and reduce their effectiveness at receiving wind from all directions [28]. Additional details on Savonius turbine optimization and augmentation can be found in Appendix C. 

## **3.3.3 Hybrid Turbines** 

Hybrid turbines combine Darrieus and Savonius rotors into one VAWT primarily to address the shortcomings of both turbines including the low starting torque of the Darrieus rotor and the low coefficient of power of the Savonius rotor. Hybrid turbines are gaining popularity within urban environments and off-grid applications as they have the potential to operate effectively in lower, more variable wind conditions. Hybrid turbines typically consist of an outer Darrieus, which has high efficiency at high wind speeds but has a high cut in speed, with an inner Savonius rotor to provide torque at low wind speeds, as shown below in Fig. 9.

## **6.2.2 Set-Up** 

The first step in setting up a CFD model for VAWTs is to import the turbine geometry from a CAD software and define a computational domain, which is the region of fluid flow to be simulated. This computational domain includes the turbine as well as a large portion of the surrounding environment and must be large enough to minimize the impact of artificial boundaries on the flow around the turbine. The computational domain is then divided into two zones: an inner rotating domain that includes the turbine and an outer, stationary domain that includes the rest of the surrounding fluid as shown in Fig.17 [38]. 

Fig. 17. CFD Flow volume with stationary and rotating domains 

The rotation of the turbine can be handled in one of two ways: through physically rotating the inner domain at each time step in a transient study using an Arbitrary Mesh Interface (AMI), or through using a rotating reference frame to approximate the rotation in a steady state study using a technique known as Multiple Reference Frames (MRF) [39]. In either case, the inner domain is rotated at a prescribed angular velocity and will rotate at that angular velocity regardless of the wind speed or the aerodynamic forces on the blades. 

Another key aspect of CFD set-up is appropriately choosing a turbulence model. Turbulence is characterized by chaotic, irregular flow occurring across diverse spatial and temporal scales. The free-stream air velocity is often turbulent, particularly in urban environments. In addition, as the fluid interacts with the rotating blades, it develops turbulent characteristics as boundary layers form, potentially separate, and undergo a transition from laminar to turbulent flow [38]. These factors make it necessary to treat the flow as turbulent. While directly resolving turbulence is possible, it requires numerically solving turbulent flows at a large range of temporal and spatial scales, making it nearly impossible for engineering applications. Instead, engineers use simplified turbulence models that are less computationally expensive, but still predict the main impact of turbulence on the flow [40]. Choosing an appropriate turbulence model is an important decision to balance accuracy and computational cost. We used the k-omega SST turbulence model for all our simulations.

## **6.4 Scaling** 

As mentioned previously, from our wind tunnel testing alone, we were not able to determine whether our selected concept would cut-in on a rooftop at full scale due to the influence of power electronics, unsteady and multidirectional wind conditions, and differences in scaling. One aspect we could effectively study was the impact of scaling. VAWTs minimize both noise and vibration as compared to HAWTs on a similar scale. Noise emitted from wind turbines can be divided into mechanical and aerodynamic noise, with the mechanical noise coming from the gearbox and the mechanics of the system, while the aerodynamic noise is due to the wind force on the blades and the movement of the blades through the fluid. For HAWTs, most of the aerodynamic noise is generated by the wing tip as the blade travels around the shaft [A1]. Because VAWT blades are moving slower compared to HAWT blades, aerodynamic noise is typically lower for VAWTs. 

In a study performed by Möllerström et. al. [A2], numerical methods were used to simulate aerodynamic noise for a 200 kW VAWT and compare these results to similarly scaled HAWTs. They found that the noise emission at 10 m above the ground while operating at optimum tip speeds, at wind speeds of 6 m/s and 8 m/s, to be 94.1dBA and 96.2dBA respectively. These emissions fall at the absolute lower range of similar size HAWTs of 95.1–100.2 dBA and 97.3–102.4 dBA for 6 m/s and 8 m/s respectively [A2]. 

In another study, by Larsson et al [A3], initial noise measurements were performed on a 200kW vertical axis wind turbine (VAWT) and results were compared to a similar size horizontal axis wind turbine (HAWT). The results from the propagation measurements indicated that noise declines more rapidly with distance for the VAWT than for the HAWT, as illustrated in Fig. A1, where the VAWT (red) exhibits a steeper decline in sound intensity than the HAWT (blue). The design additionally boasts strong connections for the Darrius blades via connecting brackets. 

Fig. D7. Notes 1 and 4 indicate where the Darrius blades connect into the center pole. Notes 2 and 3 illustrate the directions the Savonius blades face. [D10] 

We chose the DS3000 for in depth investigation because it is ICC (International Code Council) certified and boasts of being commercially available, with installations all over the world [D12]. It was the first vertical axis wind turbine to be ICC certified in the US in 2019. An additional reason we investigated this design is that hybrids, which combine Savonius and Darrieus in one turbine, are one of the more promising options in small wind power [D13]. There are many different hybrid models using different sizes and types of each design, this is one successful example representing a wide range of hybrids. 

The major insights gained from the investigation of the DS3000 is in its hybrid nature. The Savonius aspect allows it to capture wind at low speeds, while the Darrieus aspect increases the power generation capabilities of the turbine at higher wind speeds [D10]. The use of a 

multi-stage (or multi-layered) Savonius is innovative in a hybrid and could be something worth exploring as we move further into the project. One potential drawback of hybrids that is worth exploring is whether the outer Darrieus damages the ability of the inner Savonius to capture wind and maximize energy generation at low wind speeds, since this is the range at which our turbine will be operating.

The UGE-4K Vertical Axis Wind Turbine (Fig. D8) is a helical-bladed Darrieus-type model developed by Urban Green Energy (UGE) for distributed and urban wind generation.

## **Additional Considerations** 

When installing VAWTs in urban environments, several site-specific factors must be considered to ensure efficient performance and structural feasibility. Prior studies suggest that an average wind speed of approximately 5.5 m/s is desirable for efficient turbine operation, however, this value should be treated as a guideline rather than a strict requirement [8]. Urban wind conditions vary significantly and may still support viable energy generation below this threshold depending on our turbine design and placement. 

To reduce turbulence and improve energy capture, the turbine hub is often recommended to be positioned above surrounding structures, with some studies suggesting heights on the order of 30% above the rooftop and above the turbulent boundary layer [8]. In practice, these recommendations must be balanced against campus-specific constraints, including architectural integration and maximum allowable structure height, as outlined in the design requirements. High-rise buildings are generally more suitable sites, as they experience stronger and steadier wind flows, while positioning VAWTs near roof edges can take advantage of upward-inclined wind patterns where the turbulent layer is thinner [8]. Smaller-scale turbines also tend to perform better on towers than on low-rise rooftops, where wind speed amplification is typically lower [8]. In addition to aerodynamic considerations, practical factors such as building load capacity, vibration transmission, maintenance accessibility, and potential noise or visual impacts must alsio be evaluated prior to installation.

## **Savonius** 

Savonius VAWTs are drag-based turbines that typically consist of two curved blades, one convex and one concave as shown in Fig. 2. The working concept for Savonius VAWTs is a difference in drag between the convex and concave blades which causes the turbine to rotate. Savonius VAWTs face challenges related to low power generation and efficiency because certain portions of the rotation produce negative torque. A wide range of these concepts aim to address this 

challenge, found in research papers and commercial technology, are listed in Table II. These primarily include changes to blade design and external augmentation to direct windflow, with the goal of improving turbine efficiency, increasing net power output, and reducing the negative torque experienced during rotation.

## **Conclusion** 

This memo compiled VAWT concepts from research studies, patents, and commercial technologies, as well as original ideas generated by VAWT  Ventures team members, providing descriptions, levels of development, and relevant images for each concept. While the concepts are organized into Darrieus, Savonius, and hybrid categories, there is potential to combine features across these configurations to create new, more effective designs. In particular, hybrid designs offer a promising pathway because they can leverage the efficiency of lift-based Darrieus turbines while incorporating the self-starting capability of drag-based Savonius turbines, mitigating the drawbacks of each individual type. Building on documented concepts, the team should prioritize hybridization studies that combine aspects of lift-based and drag-based turbines, specifically mixed configurations.

## **Concept Evaluation** 

Throughout the organization of the concepts, the team did additional research to define each concept enough to begin evaluation. After organization and preliminary research, we did an initial pass of all the sorted concepts as individuals to assess whether they were feasible for the project. We each identified “infeasible” concepts from our own perspectives, so when we came together as a group, we could agree upon a shared definition of feasibility. To us, feasibility captured intuitive decisions of what would be capable of generating power comparable to other designs and what was outside our modeling capabilities within the year. A few concepts removed from this section were “a single turbine that runs on lift and drag without passing the limit of a hybrid,” “a form of hybrid where the entire turbine switches skeleton (from Savonius to Darrieus and visa versa) depending on wind speed,” and “the original Persian VAWT.” In addition to edits made based on feasibility, we decided to remove any concepts from the Exterior Augmentation category that would affect the multidirectionality of the wind turbine, therefore damaging the ability of the rotor to produce power from wind in multiple directions. This narrowed our set to around 170 concepts. 

From there, we decided that this large number of concepts was out of our scope and difficult to evaluate in comparison to one another. We focused on the Skeleton of VAWT category because the other categories were less fundamental to the base level design of the VAWT and made changes too small to be evaluated. We removed the concepts in the following categories: Internal Augmentation, External Augmentation, Blade Shape, and Other Optimization. Narrowing to the Skeleton of a VAWT category reduced our set to roughly 30 concepts. 

The next winnowing process was determined by individual evaluation matrices. Each person evaluated 11 concepts; 3 to 5 of those concepts overlapped with another member's concepts to account for personal biases. The evaluation was on a scale from 1 being the lowest (passable) to 5 being the highest (ideal), and 0 was given to any values that we had no information on. The evaluation criteria used were “Cut-in Speed,” “Expectation of Power Generation in low wind speeds,” “Excitement to Pursue,” “Modeling and Prototyping Confidence,” and “Rotor Height.” Table II is the evaluation matrix the team used to evaluate the 30 concepts.

## **Conclusion and Next Steps** 

In this memo, we presented our initial concept evaluation techniques, the winnowing process we used to reduce more than two hundred ideas into a manageable set, and the four final concepts we selected to move into the next phase. By organizing all concepts into categories, removing duplicates, and focusing our evaluation on Skeletons of VAWTs, we ensured that our comparisons centered on fundamental structural variations rather than secondary optimizations. 

Our multi-stage evaluation process incorporated feasibility, availability of information, expected performance at low wind speeds, simplicity, and team excitement, gradually narrowing the field from 224 concepts to eight. With this smaller set, we were able to make more technically 

informed decisions using cut-in speeds, power generation curves, and relative risk levels. From these eight, we selected four concepts that balance feasibility, diversity of ideas, excitement, and potential for optimization. 

These four concepts represent the designs we believe are the most viable for further analysis. Moving forward, we will model each design, explore parameter optimizations, and begin determining which concept offers the strongest combination of power output, simplicity, and long-term suitability for our stakeholder needs.

## **CFD Best Practices for VAWTs** 

In general, it is necessary to use 3D simulations for VAWTs because 2D simulations fail to capture flow phenomena that impact VAWT aerodynamic performance. In particular, 2D studies ignore tip losses and parasitic drag from support status causing them to overpredict peak CP by 30% to 50% compared to 3D simulations and experimental data [1]. 2D simulations can still be useful for qualitative trend identification and preliminary design comparative assessments, but 3D analysis is necessary for accurate performance prediction [1]. 

The size of the outer domain is critical to minimize the influence of artificial boundaries on the flow around the turbine and its wake development. Another key aspect of CFD set-up is appropriately choosing a turbulence model. Turbulence is characterized by chaotic, irregular flow occurring across diverse spatial and temporal scales. The free-stream air velocity is often turbulent, particularly in urban environments. In addition, as the fluid interacts with the rotating blades, it develops turbulence characteristics as boundary layers form, potentially separate and undergo a transition from laminar to turbulent flow [1]. These factors make it necessary to treat the flow as turbulent. While directly resolving turbulence is possible, it requires numerically solving turbulent flows at a large range of temporal and spatial scales, making it nearly impossible for engineering applications. Instead, engineers use simplified turbulence models that are less computationally expensive, but still predict the main impact of turbulence on the flow [2]. Choosing an appropriate turbulence model is an important decision to balance accuracy and computational cost. 

There are two main families of turbulence models: Reynolds averaged Navier Stoke (RANS) and Large eddy simulations (LES). RANS models focus on time-averaged flow properties and then model the effect of all turbulent eddies. RANS models are fast and computationally inexpensive, making them useful for applications where large-scale turbulent structures are not the primary interest. However, they are unsuitable for applications where flow instabilities, detailed vortex 

shedding, or transient phenomena are critical [3]. Three common types of RANS models are k-epsilon (k-ε), k-omega (k–ω), and k–ω Shear Stress Transport (SST). K-epsilon is good when flow is fully turbulent and far from the walls or when computational cost is a major constraint. However, it is typically not suitable for VAWTs because of its inability to accurately model flows with strong pressure gradients and boundary layer separation [1]. K-omega models are preferred for wall-bounded functions and flows with strong boundary layer effects, adverse pressure gradients, or mild flow separation. They resolve flow all the way to the wall, but require a much finer mesh in the near wall region, increasing its computational cost [3]. The k–ω Shear Stress Transport (SST) model uses k-omega modeling in the inner parts of the boundary layer and switches to k-epsilon in the free-stream to avoid the k-omega model’s sensitivity to freestream turbulence properties. It also contains a modification to improve its predictions of flow separation and pressure gradients [3]. K-omega SST is widely regarded as the most suitable RANS model for VAWT simulations as it can accurately predict power coefficients and torque and provides reasonable agreement with experimental data and wake characteristics across various TSRs [1]. It is recommended for initial design exploration and parametric studies, capturing the focus on our project. 

In contrast to RANS models, LES simulations spatially filter out the smallest scales of turbulence and resolve the large scales of turbulence with the most energy directly. LES models are much higher fidelity than RANS models and capture transient flow structures, but they are much higher computational cost and require very fine meshes and small time steps to resolve large eddies. LES models are well suited for acoustics, combustion, and flows with large separation where RANS models fail [3]. Due to their high computational costs, pure LES models are often used only in fundamental research, benchmarking studies, or simulations with simple geometry [1]. 

In addition to pure RANS and LES models, there are hybrid models that aim to reduce computational cost of LES while maintaining some of the increased accuracy. One example is Detached Eddy Simulation (DES) which uses RANS in the boundary layer near the walls where it is efficient and accurate and switches to LES in regions away from the wall where the flow separates and large, unsteady eddies form [3]. Hybrid models reflect ongoing efforts to resolve time-dependent features like dynamics stall and wake dynamics while minimizing computational cost [1]. ## **Suggested FEA Workflow** 

FEA can be performed using the Simulation workspace in Fusion  [1]. 

Recommended process: 

1. Perform analytical stress calculations to estimate initial dimensions 

2. Build a simplified CAD model of structural components 

3. Run static stress analysis to validate stresses and deflections 

4. Conduct modal analysis to evaluate vibration behavior 

5. Refine geometry or materials based on simulation results 

FEA should be used primarily to validate analytical calculations, rather than as the sole method of determining design parameters. 

## **Recommended Structural Analysis Areas** 

## **1. Blade Structural Analysis** 

The turbine blades will experience several loading conditions during operation and must be designed to withstand both aerodynamic and rotational forces. 

Key considerations: 

- Bending stresses from aerodynamic lift and drag forces 

- Centripetal forces due to blade rotation 

- Potential torsional stresses from uneven loading 

- Optimal blade thickness and internal structure 

Recommendations: 

- Determine blade stresses using basic solid mechanics equations before performing FEA 

- Consider hollow blade structures to reduce weight while maintaining stiffness 

   - The current small-scale VAWT models use solid blades, so the blade geometry may need to be modified when scaling the design in Fusion 

Relevant precedent: 

A capstone project at Memorial University designed an H-rotor Darrieus turbine and used: 

- Analytical bending and torsion calculations 

- Composite laminate analysis 

- FEA validation of blade stresses 

Their methodology provides a useful example workflow for validating turbine blade structural performance [2]. 

## **2. Shaft and Rotor Support Analysis** 

The central shaft transfers loads from the rotor to the generator and support structure. 

Key considerations: 

- Bending stresses caused by aerodynamic loads 

- Torsional stress from transmitted torque 

- Shaft deflection under loading 

- Interaction between the shaft and blade support struts 

Recommendations: 

- Estimate loads using aerodynamic forces obtained from CFD simulations 

- Use bending stress equations to determine preliminary shaft diameter 

- Perform FEA to verify shaft stresses and deflection 

## **3. Blade-Strut Attachment** 

The structural connections between the blades and the shaft must transfer loads efficiently. 

Key considerations: 

- Bending stresses in the struts 

- Load transfer between blade, strut, and shaft 

Recommendations: 

- Conduct simplified beam calculations before validating designs with FEA 

## **4. Vibration and Resonance Analysis** 

Wind turbines operate under cyclic loading, making vibration analysis critical. 

Key considerations: 

- Natural frequency of blades, shaft, and support structure 

- Potential resonance with operating rotor speeds 

- Structural stability of rotating components 

Recommended approach: 

1. Determine expected turbine operating speed 

2. Calculate the frequency at which aerodynamic and centripetal loads vary during blade rotation 

3. Perform modal analysis using FEA to determine natural frequencies 

4. Compare natural frequencies with operating frequencies to ensure resonance is avoided 

## **5. Critical Speed of the Drive Shaft** 

Rotating shafts may experience instability at certain speeds due to imbalance or eccentricity. 

Key considerations: 

- Critical shaft speed 

- Shaft deflection and dynamic stability 

- Generator coupling effects 

Recommendations: 

- Determine the expected operational rotational speed 

- Calculate the shaft’s critical speed and ensure it remains well above operational speed 

## **Research and Validation Recommendations** 

Structural analysis should be supported by existing literature and validated modeling methods. Recommended research areas include: 

- Structural design of Darrieus and Savonius turbines 

- Composite turbine blade structures 

- Vibration behavior of rotating shafts 

- Rooftop wind turbine structural considerations 

Validation of results can be achieved through: 

- Comparison with published turbine designs 

- Sensitivity analyses on material properties and loads 

- Experimental testing of prototype components 

## **Conclusion** 

The next phase team should approach structural analysis through a combination of analytical calculations, FEA validation, and comparison with existing turbine research. This multi-step process will help ensure the turbine is structurally feasible before prototype construction.

## **Executive Summary** 

As interest in sustainable energy grows, distributed energy technologies are becoming increasingly important for reducing reliance on centralized power grids. In collaboration with 99P Labs at Honda Research Institute (HRI), this project explores vertical axis wind turbines (VAWTs) as a potential solution for urban renewable energy generation. While VAWTs are well-suited for low-speed, turbulent, and multidirectional wind conditions, they remain underdeveloped compared to horizontal axis wind turbines (HAWTs). 

This project represents phase 1 of a multiphase effort to design a rooftop-compatible VAWT for application on McConnell Hall at Smith College. This phase focused on concept development, small-scale prototyping, and preliminary performance evaluation to establish a foundation for future design and implementation. 

Our design process began with the exploration of over 200 VAWT concepts from commercial technologies, academic literature, and original ideation. Through structured screening and evaluation, this was narrowed to four concepts, which were modeled, 3D printed, and tested in a 12 in. × 12 in. wind tunnel. Based on experimental results and iterative refinement, a helical hybrid configuration, combining a helical Darrieus rotor with an internal Savonius rotor, was selected for its balance of startup capability and rotational performance. 

Performance evaluation integrated wind tunnel testing, rooftop wind data analysis, computational fluid dynamics (CFD), wake comparison, and scaling. These methods provided insight into turbine behavior while highlighting key limitations. Challenges in CFD verification and discrepancies between modeled and experimental behavior revealed the need for higher-fidelity analysis, and scaling results emphasized the difficulty of translating small-scale testing to real-world urban environments. 

An initial economic analysis showed that the current design is not viable for rooftop energy generation due to low predicted power output and an impractical payback period. Despite this, the project establishes a foundation for future development through a structured design process, validated experimental and computational workflows, and identification of key challenges in efficiency, startup behavior, scaling, and validation. Recommended next steps include improving the wind tunnel setup to allow for more in-depth data collection and further optimizing turbine performance, exploring alternative application environments with higher wind speeds, or advancing CFD toward a high-fidelity digital twin. 

This project contributes to the early-stage development of VAWTs as a distributed energy technology and supports HRI/99P Labs’ broader mission to explore innovative, sustainability-driven solutions.

## **5 Design Development** 

This section describes the process used to generate, evaluate, and refine potential VAWT designs. We began by exploring a wide range of commercial and theoretical concepts and narrowed these options through a structured concept selection process. The most promising concepts were then modeled in Fusion, 3D printed, and tested in the wind tunnel to evaluate performance. Results from these experiments informed subsequent refinement and design selection. 

## **5.1 Concept Generation and Organization** 

We began the design process by exploring the existing design space of VAWTs. During this stage, our goal was to identify a wide range of possible concepts rather than narrowing the design space prematurely. 

Once we had gained sufficient technical foundation on Darrieus, Savonius, and Hybrid VAWTs, we compiled a list of 33 VAWTs being sold commercially to determine which types of design have been successful as well as information on size, power electronics, and expected power output. From this list of 33 VAWTs, we chose five designs to study in more depth based on the availability of information as well as attempting to cover the variety of VAWTs on the market. A specific discussion of our methodology and the 5 chosen turbines can be found in Appendix D. 

Overall, we compiled VAWT concepts from several sources, including commercial technologies, academic research papers, patents, and original ideas generated by team members. This broad exploration allowed us to capture a diverse set of aerodynamic approaches to VAWT design. In total, we documented over 200 VAWT concepts as can be seen in Fig. X. 

Fig. X. Design Space Concept Organization [K0] 

After compiling the full set of concepts, we organized them into design categories to simplify comparison and evaluation. These categories included: 

- Skeleton of a VAWT (overall turbine configuration) 

- Blade Shape 

- Interior Augmentation 

- Exterior Augmentation 

- Other Optimization 

The Skeleton of a VAWT category described the fundamental turbine configuration, such as Darrieus rotors, Savonius rotors, or hybrid rotor configurations. The remaining categories represented modifications to specific VAWT components. 

During this organization stage, we removed duplicate concepts, combined closely related ideas, and eliminated entries that were not standalone concepts (such as material selections). This process reduced redundancy and clarified relationships between different design approaches. A more detailed description of our concept generation methodology can be found in Appendix E [Concept Generation Memo].

## **5.2 Initial Concept Screening** 

After organizing the design space, we began a winnowing process to identify concepts suitable for further analysis. We evaluated each concept for feasibility within the scope of this phase, with particular emphasis on manufacturability (DC-03), compatibility with Smith College architecture (DC-05), and power generation (DC-06). Concepts were removed if they were too complex to realistically manufacture or model at this stage, required external starting mechanisms that would complicate implementation, or were unlikely to generate meaningful power under low and turbulent wind conditions. Additionally, designs that compromised the VAWT’s ability to operate omnidirectionally were eliminated, as this would limit performance in variable urban wind environments. 

Following this feasibility screening, we further reduced the remaining concepts by focusing on the Skeleton of a VAWT category. We determined that variations in VAWT skeleton would have a larger impact on performance than smaller design changes such as blade shape or minor augmentations. Limiting the design space to skeleton configurations helped us reduce our list to approximately 30 concepts. 

## **5.3 Concept Selection** 

To further narrow the design space, we evaluated the remaining concepts using an evaluation matrix. Each team member independently evaluated a subset of the concepts using criteria derived from stakeholder needs and design considerations. 

The evaluation criteria included: 

- Expected power generation in low wind speeds 

- Modeling and prototyping feasibility 

- Rotor height compatibility with rooftop applications 

- Team interest in pursuing the concept 

We evaluated some of the concepts using reported cut-in speed values when that information was available. However, because many published concepts did not provide this parameter, we ultimately incorporated it into the broader criterion of expected power generation in low wind conditions. 

We scored each concept on a five-point scale ranging from passable to ideal. After completing individual evaluations, the team compared results and discussed discrepancies to reduce bias and develop a shared understanding of the strengths and limitations of each design. 

Through the iterative winnowing and evaluation process described in Appendix F [Concept Selection Memo], we reduced the design space from over 200 initial concepts to a final set of four concepts. Selecting four concepts allowed each team member to take primary responsibility for developing and analyzing one design while maintaining collaboration across the team. These concepts represent a range of VAWT configurations that balance feasibility, expected performance in low wind conditions, and potential for further development. 

These prototypes, shown in Fig. X, served as the foundation for the experimental evaluation described in the following sections. 

Fig. X. Classical Savonius [K1], EN0005 [K2], Helical Savonius Within a Helical Darrieus [K3], and H-Rotor Darrieus With Savonius Compartments [K4] 

We selected these four concepts for initial small scale prototyping and experimental evaluation. Each configuration represented a distinct approach to VAWT design, allowing us to compare different aerodynamic methods for initiating rotation in low wind speeds.

## **5.4 Prototype development** 

We translated each of the four selected turbine concepts into CAD models using Fusion and fabricated them as small-scale 3D printed prototypes. We designed a common central attachment to connect with two ball bearings at the top and bottom of the prototype, allowing it to sit upon a metal rod within the wind tunnel and ensure that each prototype could freely rotate during testing. We chose prototype dimensions of 5 inches tall x 4.5 inches wide to maintain consistent swept areas across all designs, allowing fair performance comparisons. 

## **5.5 Wind Tunnel Testing** 

We conducted wind tunnel testing to evaluate each prototype’s ability to initiate rotation under low wind speeds using the small-scale 3D printed prototypes. 

We mounted each prototype on a vertical metal rod inside the wind tunnel test section, using two ball bearings to minimize friction and allow the prototype to rotate freely during testing, as can be seen in Fig. X. We increased the wind tunnel speed incrementally in steps of 0.1 Hz starting at 0.4 Hz. 

Fig. X. Wind Tunnel Testing Setup 

At each wind speed increment, we observed the turbine behavior and recorded one of three states: 

- No Spin: the turbine remained stationary 

- Some Spin: intermittent or inconsistent rotation occurred 

- Constant Spin: the turbine rotated continuously 

We recorded the wind speed at which continuous rotation first occurred as the cut-in speed for that prototype, and we tested each prototype twice to ensure consistent results. 

During this testing phase, the Darrieus with Savonius Compartments design was unable to cut-in and lacked sufficient support in existing literature to justify further optimization within the project timeline. As a result, we removed this concept from further development. Further testing focused on the three remaining turbine configurations: the Classical Savonius, EN0005, and the Helical Hybrid. Across multiple rounds of testing, we evaluated several prototype variations of 

these three concepts that incorporated aerodynamic augmentations identified through literature review. These augmentations included modifications to blade geometry and structural features intended to improve startup behavior and rotational stability, as can be seen in Fig. X. All of the 3D-printed prototypes are also shown in Appendix G [Prototyping Roadmap]. 

Fig. X. Prototyping Optimization Roadmap 

After each testing round, we selected the best-performing prototypes for additional refinement, updating the CAD and reprinting them for further testing. Because of project time constraints, we limited the number of optimization iterations, and as a result, the selected prototype represents the most promising configuration tested rather than a fully optimized VAWT design.
