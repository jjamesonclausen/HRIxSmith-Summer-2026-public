**Concept Selection Process**
After defining our design goal we moved onto the concept selection phase to determine which VAWT designs or design concepts in the wiki would be best suited to use as a starting point for optimization. To begin narrowing the list of 63 VAWT designs in the wiki, both Julie and Anna did a first pass of the wiki, with LLM assistance, and selected 8-10 promising designs based on their perceived potential for efficacy at BOS, and if they were interesting concepts to pursue. After comparing the initial design lists, we agreed upon six designs to evaluate further. A list of selection criteria was developed based on our design goal, and used to score these potential VAWT designs. The criteria used were "Startup ability" (with a goal of`<= 3 m/s`), "Efficiency," "Rated speed" (goal: `10-15 m/s`), "Cut-out speed" (goal: `<= 20 m/s`), "Room for Growth" (indicating potential to iterate upon the design), "CAD & Manufacturing Feasibility," and "Team Interest". Anna and Julie first manually scored the designs before asking the LLM to repeat the exercise. The human and LLM scores were very similar for most categories, especially for quantitative criteria. The LLM scores for qualitative criteria such as difficulty to model in CAD software or interest in pursuing, were replaced with the average of Anna and Julie's scores. After scoring, we weighted the criteria based on their importance to our design goal and project feasibility and ranked the designs.

In order to perform a sensitivity analysis of our scores and weights, we prompted the LLM to rank the designs using three different weighting schemes based on different 'personas'. These were a beginner/student engineer with minimal CAD or CFD experience, an expert engineer with extensive CAD and CFD experience, and an engineer whose primary goal was simply to produce the most optimal VAWT for BOS, regardless of constraints. After ranking the six selected designs with these weights the same two designs repeatedly came out on top, validating our selection process. This sensitivity test also helped make clear the relative strengths and weaknesses of the top two designs which further informed our next steps. Before moving on we used the LLM to apply the ranking system to all 63 designs in the wiki to ensure that our initial pass hadn't prematurely eliminated any designs. We found that H-type VAWT designs made up a large proportion of the top-ranked designs with the previously identified best designs remaining the top two. The two designs selected to move forward were a dual-phase H-type VAWT, and a unique Darrieus VAWT, pictured below. In order to decide which of the two designs to begin testing, both were moved forward into the CAD phase to gauge modeling feasibility.

The use of AI significantly accelerated the process of reviewing research papers and assessing VAWT designs. The LLM could quickly retrieve and compare information across the entire wiki which was extremely helpful for both human-driven design exploration and for AI-driven filters and rankings. AI helped identify candidate concepts, extract structured design data, compile comparison tables, and calculate weighted scores. This made it possible to go from a large set of designs to selecting two within a single afternoon. However, after having completed this process we noticed that the efficiency cost us the deep learning that can really only be achieved through time spent engaging with the material directly. For this reason we endeavored to take advantage of the quick information retrieval capabilities of the LLM while remaining actively involved in manually assessing top designs and using our own judgement to structure the process and review all LLM recommendations. This was also essential to ensure that the work done by AI was fully accurate, especially when sources were less clear about their outcomes or reported them primarily through figures. 

**CAD**
 As per the AI-exploration objective of this project, we explored two CAD AI tools: Zoo Design Studio's Zookeeper, and Fusion's Autodesk Assistant. 

We used the LLM wiki to generate prompts for the CAD AI agents based on the design specifications in the research papers for the two selected VAWT designs. The dual-phase H-type design was very well described so the AI prompt was quite robust and very successful. The unique Darrieus design was harder to design due to a lack of specifications in the source paper. 






# AI Outlines

## Design Goal

### Design-goal process

1. **Choose a site where the problem could be made concrete.** Anna and Julie narrowed the project from VAWTs in general to a proposed installation on Governor's Island at Boston Logan International Airport. Logan was selected because accessible, high-resolution wind data could support site-specific screening, the reported average wind speed was about `5 m/s`, the airport has high energy demand, and Massport has stated decarbonization goals. (Sources: [[Design goal]], [[brainstorm]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Project Update 7_14.pdf|Project Update 7/14]])
2. **Treat the site as both an opportunity and a constraint.** The team investigated airport airspace requirements and identified a proposed Governor's Island location outside the restricted runway area. The preliminary slide concept placed the rotor center at the `9 m` measurement height, for an approximately `10 m` overall turbine, and described a roughly `200 m` runway separation; these are planning assumptions, not confirmed installation approvals. (Sources: [[Design goal]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Project Update 7_14.pdf|Project Update 7/14]])
3. **Turn wind and siting evidence into design priorities.** The goal was a VAWT that can use the site's low-to-moderate wind resource through low cut-in behavior, rated and cut-out behavior suited to BOS conditions, competitive efficiency, and credible economic payback. It also had to avoid unnecessary complexity, need little maintenance, comply with airport constraints, and remain manufacturable, modelable in Zoo, and compatible with sound CFD practice within the project timeline. (Sources: [[Design goal]], [[brainstorm]], [[Decision Making Process]], [[Project Update 7_14.pdf|Project Update 7/14]])
4. **Make the goal usable for screening without confusing it for a final specification.** The final weekly update used preliminary targets of cut-in below `3 m/s`, rated speed `7-10 m/s`, and cut-out above `15 m/s`. Earlier analysis notes instead used approximately `10-15 m/s` rated speed and cut-out at or above `20 m/s`; this shows that the operating envelope was still being refined, not locked. (Sources: [[Decision Making Process]], [[Concept ranking worksheet]], [[Project Update 7_14.pdf|Project Update 7/14]])
5. **Use the goal to sequence the work rather than declare a finished turbine.** The team used it to prioritize concept selection, a baseline CAD model, CFD validation, and then a limited parameter study. The subsequent VJ20/VA9 recommendation kept low-wind use, manufacturability, maintenance, and permitted height ahead of maximum `Cp` alone, while identifying missing height, load, target-power, and installation-point wind inputs as open constraints. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[VJ20 VA9 DOE Recommendation]], [[Project Update 7_14.pdf|Project Update 7/14]])

### How AI affected the process and outcome

- **It accelerated conversion of a broad idea into a working brief.** The LLM wiki was used to review Boston-area wind data, identify Logan and Governor's Island as a plausible context, summarize airport constraints, and generate alternative work plans. The team then compared the suggestions rather than accepting one generated plan unchanged. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Project Update 7_14.pdf|Project Update 7/14]])
- **It made evidence easier to retrieve, not automatically settled.** Searchable design and parameter metadata, wind histograms, and structured comparison notes helped connect site conditions to design criteria. The team still had to decide which constraints mattered and how much weight to give them. (Sources: [[brainstorm]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Project Update 7_7.pdf|Project Update 7/7]])
- **It exposed where the brief remained uncertain.** Different recorded rated-speed and cut-out targets, unconfirmed airport-height and clearance assumptions, and missing installation-point wind, generator/load, and target-power data mean the goal was appropriate for early screening but not yet an approved design requirements document. (Sources: [[Decision Making Process]], [[VJ20 VA9 DOE Recommendation]], [[Project Update 7_14.pdf|Project Update 7/14]])
- **It did not replace human judgment or learning.** Anna and Julie reported that AI made site and concept synthesis unusually fast, while also making it harder to gain the deeper understanding that comes from slowly working through the sources. They therefore retained human discussion and review of the project constraints, priorities, and generated plans. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Project Update 7_14.pdf|Project Update 7/14]])

## Concept Selection
### Concept selection process

1. **Define the site and design goal.** Anna and Julie selected Boston Logan International Airport as the intended context, using its wind data (average wind speed `4.8 m/s` and average gusts `6.3 m/s`) and airport constraints to frame the problem. The resulting goal prioritized low-speed startup, competitive efficiency and economics, feasible manufacture and maintenance, regulatory compliance, and a geometry that could be modeled and evaluated within the project timeline. (Sources: [[Design goal]], [[brainstorm]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])
2. **Translate the goal into selection criteria.** The team used startup ability (ideally at or below `3 m/s`), efficiency/economics, rated-speed fit, cut-out-speed fit, room for further parameter studies, CAD/manufacturing feasibility, and interest in the concept. Startup and efficiency were given the greatest weight because they mattered most for the BOS wind distribution and economic viability. (Sources: [[Decision Making Process]], [[Concept ranking worksheet]])
3. **Build and compare a broad candidate set.** The initial concepts included straight-bladed H-rotor Darrieus, helical Darrieus, reduced-interference hybrid, and J-type/Savonius-derived designs. The team then added source-backed alternatives, including the involute rotor with a wind-flow modifier, the VJ20 hybrid, the 50% shifted-troposkien VAWT, and the scooplet Savonius. (Sources: [[brainstorm]], [[Concept ranking worksheet]], [[Decision Making Process]])
4. **Organize source evidence for comparison.** Research papers were converted into a source-grounded LLM wiki with separate design and parameter pages. Structured metadata captured comparable quantities, including cut-in and cut-out speeds, rated speed and power, swept area, and maximum `Cp` in site-relevant wind-speed bands. Obsidian data views and a design-comparison table made the distributed evidence easier to retrieve and compare. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Design Comparison]], [[data view testing]])
5. **Rank, stress-test, and narrow the concepts.** Anna and Julie first ranked candidates independently, then compared their judgments with AI-generated weighted rankings. They also changed criterion weights for BOS-focused, beginner-friendly, and expert-performance personas. Across these comparisons, the self-starting H-type EN0005/VA9 and VJ20 hybrid consistently emerged as the leading concepts. The team retained both for further reading and CAD exploration rather than treating a single score as a final decision. (Sources: [[Concept ranking worksheet]], [[Decision Making Process]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])
6. **Validate the leading options before proceeding.** The team reviewed the top designs more closely, compared their BOS tradeoffs, and tested their modelability in Zoo. VJ20's reported efficiency made it attractive, while VA9 had stronger low-wind startup and simpler geometry. CAD trials also showed that VA9's paper provided less-specific geometry and was harder for Zoo to reproduce, while VJ20 was more tractable as a modeling baseline. The evolving outcome was an H-type-focused direction with VA9 and VJ20 as the two principal designs for iteration. (Sources: [[Decision Making Process]], [[Concept ranking worksheet]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])

## CAD

### CAD process

1. **Carry two leading concepts into CAD as a feasibility test.** After concept selection, Anna and Julie explored the VA9 EN0005 self-start Darrieus rotor and the VJ20 dual-H-rotor hybrid rather than assuming that a high ranking made either design readily modellable. They first reviewed the source material, identified the geometry that was actually specified, and recorded missing dimensions as assumptions rather than source facts. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD Modeling Process]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])
2. **Translate research evidence into constrained, parameterized AI-CAD prompts.** For VA9, the team supplied the published blade-body height, total rotor height, radius, chord, three-part blade arrangement, inward blade-end configuration, and EN0005 profile description, while requiring editable parameters and explicit labeling of inferred support dimensions and profile reconstruction. For VJ20, the prompt fixed the outer NACA0018 and inner DU 06-W-200 rotor geometry, blade counts, pitches, and `60 deg` phase offset, while allowing the tool to propose clearly labeled support hardware. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD log/j710_va9_initial]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20]])
3. **Generate and inspect first-pass ZooKeeper/KCL assemblies.** ZooKeeper produced multi-file parametric assemblies for both concepts. The VA9 model used separate blade-body, blade-end, support, shaft, and assembly files; the VJ20 model used concentric inner and outer H-rotors on a shared shaft. The team treated rendered views, not only successful code execution or constrained sketches, as necessary checks for disconnected, misaligned, or otherwise implausible geometry. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD log/4eed3e3d-51dc-43a6-8dc7-1b7ae205f045]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20]], [[_anna daily log]])
4. **Iterate on the geometry with focused prompts and manual code review.** The VA9 work concentrated on aligning, steepening, and smoothing the inward blade ends. ZooKeeper eventually replaced an unstable rotated-extrusion/trim approach with lofted blade-end caps, then adjusted rake and profile detail. Anna also read the KCL files and used OpenCode to troubleshoot a rotor-editing problem, building enough understanding of the model structure to make more informed changes. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD log/b46becaa-ac7e-4343-91b5-307ebca919dc]], [[_anna daily log]])
5. **Compare AI-CAD tools and simplify the prompt.** Long, detailed prompts sometimes produced glaring errors, such as disconnected or floating components. A shorter, production-oriented VJ20 prompt gave ZooKeeper a stronger initial result. The same constrained prompt was then tested in Fusion's Autodesk Assistant, which produced a visually strong full-scale and scaled VJ20 model more quickly, although later review found that its inner blades did not use the required asymmetric airfoil. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])
6. **Use CAD results to refine the design and CFD plan, without claiming validation.** The CAD trials made VJ20 a more tractable baseline because its source geometry was more fully defined, while VA9 required an approximate EN0005 profile and had an unresolved multi-body blade union. The team also separated a mechanically credible fixed-pitch assembly from a future CFD-clean model, recognizing that final source-faithful airfoils, watertight geometry, rotating/stationary regions, and independent validation were still needed before simulation results could be trusted. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD Modeling Process]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/b46becaa-ac7e-4343-91b5-307ebca919dc]])

### How AI affected the process and outcome

- **It accelerated the initial translation from papers to editable models.** AI-CAD tools turned source-backed dimensions and geometric rules into complex parametric assemblies in minutes, allowing the team to compare VA9 and VJ20 modelability early and to generate full-scale and scaled VJ20 configurations quickly. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD log/4eed3e3d-51dc-43a6-8dc7-1b7ae205f045]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20]], [[_anna daily log]])
- **It made prompt design part of the engineering work.** The team found that concise, constrained prompts worked better than overwhelming the model with detail. Explicitly separating fixed source geometry from editable inferred hardware reduced the risk that an AI-generated support choice would silently change the research-based rotor definition. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD log/j710_va9_initial]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20]], [[_anna daily log]])
- **It exposed a gap between generated geometry and user understanding.** ZooKeeper could create a complicated turbine rapidly, but follow-up edits sometimes caused missing, floating, or malformed pieces. Because Anna and Julie had not built the original geometry themselves, diagnosing those changes was difficult; reading the KCL files and asking the tool to identify modified lines helped restore some manual control. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20]])
- **It required visual and source-level verification, not trust in tool checks.** Successful execution, linting, and fully constrained sketches did not prevent disconnected VJ20 blades, floating brackets, a missing strut, an incorrect inner airfoil in Fusion, or the VA9 boolean-union failure. AI-CAD output was therefore useful as a fast starting point, but not evidence that the geometry was mechanically coherent, source-faithful, manufacturable, or CFD-ready. (Sources: [[active/documentation/CAD Modeling Process]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/b46becaa-ac7e-4343-91b5-307ebca919dc]])
- **It changed the decision process, but not the need for human judgment.** CAD experimentation added a practical criterion to the concept-selection work: available geometric detail and stable editability. VJ20 was easier to establish as a baseline, while VA9 remained valuable for its self-starting blade-end idea but needed more source geometry and robust modeling work. The remaining work is to select and independently verify a CFD-suitable geometry before using either model to draw aerodynamic conclusions. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[active/documentation/CAD Modeling Process]], [[active/documentation/CAD log/j710_va9_initial]])
