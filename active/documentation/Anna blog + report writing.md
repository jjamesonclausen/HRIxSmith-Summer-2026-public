**Concept Selection Process**
After defining our design goal we moved onto the concept selection phase to determine which VAWT designs or design concepts in the wiki would be best suited to use as a starting point for optimization. To begin narrowing the list of 63 VAWT designs in the wiki, both Julie and Anna did a first pass of the wiki, with LLM assistance, and selected 8-10 promising designs based on their perceived potential for efficacy at BOS, and if they were interesting concepts to pursue. After comparing the initial design lists, we agreed upon six designs to evaluate further. A list of selection criteria was developed based on our design goal, and used to score these potential VAWT designs. Anna and Julie first manually scored the designs before asking the LLM to repeat the exercise. The human and LLM scores were very similar for most categories, especially for quantitative criteria. The LLM scores for qualitative criteria such as difficulty to model in CAD software or interest in pursuing, were replaced with the average of Anna and Julie's scores. After scoring, we weighted the criteria based on their importance to our design goal and project feasibility and ranked the designs.
In order to perform a sensitivity analysis of our scores and weights, we prompted the LLM to rank the designs using three different weighting schemes based on different 'personas'. These were a beginner/student engineer with minimal CAD or CFD experience, an expert engineer with extensive CAD and CFD experience, and an engineer whose primary goal was simply to produce the most optimal VAWT for BOS, regardless of constraints. After ranking the six selected designs with these weights the same two designs repeatedly came out on top, validating our selection process. To ensure that our initial pass hadn't prematurely eliminated any designs, we then used the LLM to apply the same ranking system to all 63 designs in the wiki.

## AI Outline

### Concept selection process

1. **Define the site and design goal.** Anna and Julie selected Boston Logan International Airport as the intended context, using its wind data (average wind speed `4.8 m/s` and average gusts `6.3 m/s`) and airport constraints to frame the problem. The resulting goal prioritized low-speed startup, competitive efficiency and economics, feasible manufacture and maintenance, regulatory compliance, and a geometry that could be modeled and evaluated within the project timeline. (Sources: [[Design goal]], [[brainstorm]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])
2. **Translate the goal into selection criteria.** The team used startup ability (ideally at or below `3 m/s`), efficiency/economics, rated-speed fit, cut-out-speed fit, room for further parameter studies, CAD/manufacturing feasibility, and interest in the concept. Startup and efficiency were given the greatest weight because they mattered most for the BOS wind distribution and economic viability. (Sources: [[Decision Making Process]], [[Concept ranking worksheet]])
3. **Build and compare a broad candidate set.** The initial concepts included straight-bladed H-rotor Darrieus, helical Darrieus, reduced-interference hybrid, and J-type/Savonius-derived designs. The team then added source-backed alternatives, including the involute rotor with a wind-flow modifier, the VJ20 hybrid, the 50% shifted-troposkien VAWT, and the scooplet Savonius. (Sources: [[brainstorm]], [[Concept ranking worksheet]], [[Decision Making Process]])
4. **Organize source evidence for comparison.** Research papers were converted into a source-grounded LLM wiki with separate design and parameter pages. Structured metadata captured comparable quantities, including cut-in and cut-out speeds, rated speed and power, swept area, and maximum `Cp` in site-relevant wind-speed bands. Obsidian data views and a design-comparison table made the distributed evidence easier to retrieve and compare. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Design Comparison]], [[data view testing]])
5. **Rank, stress-test, and narrow the concepts.** Anna and Julie first ranked candidates independently, then compared their judgments with AI-generated weighted rankings. They also changed criterion weights for BOS-focused, beginner-friendly, and expert-performance personas. Across these comparisons, the self-starting H-type EN0005/VA9 and VJ20 hybrid consistently emerged as the leading concepts. The team retained both for further reading and CAD exploration rather than treating a single score as a final decision. (Sources: [[Concept ranking worksheet]], [[Decision Making Process]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])
6. **Validate the leading options before proceeding.** The team reviewed the top designs more closely, compared their BOS tradeoffs, and tested their modelability in Zoo. VJ20's reported efficiency made it attractive, while VA9 had stronger low-wind startup and simpler geometry. CAD trials also showed that VA9's paper provided less-specific geometry and was harder for Zoo to reproduce, while VJ20 was more tractable as a modeling baseline. The evolving outcome was an H-type-focused direction with VA9 and VJ20 as the two principal designs for iteration. (Sources: [[Decision Making Process]], [[Concept ranking worksheet]], [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])

### How AI affected the process and outcome

- **It increased the speed and scope of evidence synthesis.** The LLM wiki could retrieve and compare information across many trusted papers far faster than manual review alone. AI helped identify candidate concepts, extract structured design data, compile comparison tables, generate work-plan alternatives, analyze Logan wind data, and calculate weighted scores. This made it possible to move from a broad concept set to two leading options in about 1.5 hours. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Concept ranking worksheet]])
- **It made sensitivity testing practical.** AI supported repeated rankings with different weights and personas, revealing that VA9 was the safer option when startup, simplicity, and robustness were emphasized, while VJ20 became more attractive when performance, rated-speed fit, and optimization potential carried more weight. This made the tradeoff between practical robustness and peak performance explicit rather than hidden in a single ranking. (Sources: [[Concept ranking worksheet]], [[Decision Making Process]])
- **It supported, but did not replace, human judgment.** Anna and Julie independently ranked concepts, compared those results to the AI outputs, discussed the criteria and weights, and manually investigated the leading designs. They also used CAD experimentation as an additional feasibility check, so modelability and available geometric detail could affect the decision alongside the numerical rankings. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]], [[Decision Making Process]])
- **It exposed important reliability risks.** The team had to repeatedly correct source-ingestion, formatting, image, metadata, and link errors. More importantly, the LLM sometimes inferred quantitative values from general context instead of reporting only source-supported data. A later manual review found that it had misreported VJ20's cut-in speed, which could have distorted the ranking. This reinforced that fluent AI output and computed scores require source-level verification, especially when data are missing, graph-based, or conflicting. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])
- **It changed the learning tradeoff.** AI reduced the time spent locating and comparing research, but Anna and Julie noted that handing technical synthesis to the wiki could reduce the slower, deeper learning that comes from reading and reasoning through each source. The final process therefore treated the AI as a rapid, source-grounded research assistant and decision-analysis tool, while preserving human review of criteria, evidence quality, rankings, and the final concepts. (Sources: [[_anna daily log]], [[_julie_daily_log]])
