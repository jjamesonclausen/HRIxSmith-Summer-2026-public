# Building a VAWT in CAD With AI: Fast First Models, Slow Verification

## The CAD problem was larger than drawing a turbine

We did not begin our vertical-axis wind turbine project with a complete engineering drawing that we could reproduce in a conventional CAD package. We began with research papers, figures, partial dimensions, two selected reference designs, limited prior CAD experience, and an interest in seeing whether AI-assisted modeling could accelerate the early design process. We focused less on pressing "generate" and more on deciding what we knew, what we had to label as an assumption, and how we could tell whether a convincing-looking model was actually usable. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md)

We record our process here; we do not claim that we produced a manufacturable or aerodynamically validated turbine. We used the models as exploratory design artifacts and possible starting points for later CFD preparation. We still need to inspect the geometry independently and validate any CFD result against a suitable reference case. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md)

## Choosing designs before modeling them

Before making CAD, we used our source-grounded wiki to compare VAWT concepts against the project site, expected wind conditions, performance goals, and practical limits. We used weighted decision tables, personal rankings, and rankings from different perspectives, including an experienced designer and a beginner with limited engineering and CAD experience. Repeated comparisons led us to carry the VA9 EN0005 self-start Darrieus turbine and the VJ20 dual-H-rotor hybrid forward as the two designs to explore. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md)

We found the speed of that selection both exciting and uncomfortable. Julie recorded that narrowing the options in roughly an hour and a half felt "pretty insane," while Anna noted that handing slow comparison work to an LLM could also hand away part of the learning that normally comes from working through the source material directly. We carried that concern into CAD because we knew that a model built from an unexamined summary could look precise while preserving a misunderstanding from the very first prompt. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md)

We therefore made prompt records part of the deliverable. Instead of treating an AI-generated model as an unexplained output, we saved the source-backed dimensions, the prompt, the inferred hardware choices, and the later corrections in a CAD log. This gave us a trail from research evidence to model decisions and made it possible to distinguish a documented dimension from a convenient guess. (source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/j710_va9_initial.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md)

## Learning the tools before asking them for a turbine

Our first tool was Zoo Design and its ZooKeeper agent. We installed it, connected it to GitHub, worked through tutorials, experimented with basic shapes and windmill-like forms, and began learning KCL, the platform's parametric CAD language. Those early exercises were important because they made the later generated models less opaque: when something broke, we at least had a starting point for reading the model structure rather than only trying another prompt. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md)

ZooKeeper let us test an ambitious promise: generate a parameterized turbine assembly from a written brief. It created complicated geometry much faster than we could have modeled it manually at our initial skill level. But we learned early that speed and editability are different qualities. Julie worried that a tool able to make a whole turbine quickly might not create a sufficiently stable underlying structure for later edits. We confirmed that concern when a request to change one acceptable part altered another and made manual repair difficult until we asked the tool to report which KCL lines it had modified. (source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md)

We found that shorter prompts generally produced better first attempts than huge, overly detailed instructions. In Anna's words, long prompts could create "glaring very-ai-style errors like blades that aren't connected to anything." We did not conclude that concise prompts are universally better. Instead, we learned to separate fixed geometry, editable parameters, prohibited features, and unresolved assumptions so that the model could not satisfy the language of the request while violating our engineering intent. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md)

## VA9: reconstructing a design from incomplete evidence

We found VA9 harder to model because the published material did not provide a complete CAD-ready description. We had source-backed values for a `36.0 cm` blade-body height, `48.0 cm` rotor height, `17.3 cm` rotor radius, and `5.3 cm` chord. The source also described each blade as a main body plus two blade ends and reported that inward-positioned ends improved standstill drag and self-starting. We did not have a clean coordinate table for the EN0005 blade profile, every hidden structural dimension, or a definitive blade-end angle. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/j710_va9_initial.md)

We shaped the prompt around that absence. We did not claim an exact reconstruction. We asked for a parametric assembly in which we could revise rotor radius, height, chord, blade-end angle, blade-end length, and blade count, while we labeled missing support geometry and the airfoil reconstruction as inferred. ZooKeeper/KCL separated the initial model into parameters, blade bodies, blade ends, supports, a shaft, and an overall assembly. That structure made our uncertainty visible and kept the source-backed rotor geometry distinct from guessed hardware. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/j710_va9_initial.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/4eed3e3d-51dc-43a6-8dc7-1b7ae205f045.md)

We spent most of our VA9 effort on the blade ends. We wanted them to resemble the source's cup-like, inward-oriented concept while joining the main blade smoothly enough to look and behave like a continuous aerodynamic feature rather than a flat tab. When the original rotated-extrusion-and-trim approach became unstable during alignment changes, we switched to lofted end caps whose root profiles matched the main blade tips. We tested multiple inward rake angles, added intermediate profiles to smooth the loft, changed the loft behavior when the middle appeared to dip inward, adjusted cup depth, tested and rejected a wider outer lip, and increased the profile trace from nine to eighteen constrained segments. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/b46becaa-ac7e-4343-91b5-307ebca919dc.md)

We used VA9 to see what AI-CAD made easier and what it did not solve. The tool accelerated our creation of variants, but we still used visual review to decide whether we accepted a change. We retained a `46 deg` inward rake and smoother lofted transitions in the final logged VA9 version, but we still treat it as an approximation digitized from a figure, not an authoritative EN0005 coordinate set. The denser profile also caused the boolean union of the blade body and two ends to fail, so we retained touching but separate bodies instead of one fused solid. We learned that a model can look connected in a render while remaining unsuitable for manufacturing or downstream CFD operations that require a robust watertight solid. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/b46becaa-ac7e-4343-91b5-307ebca919dc.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/j710_va9_initial.md)

## VJ20: a stronger specification, different failures

We began VJ20 with a more complete source specification. We modeled its outer rotor with three NACA0018 blades and its inner rotor with three DU 06-W-200 asymmetric-airfoil blades. We placed both straight-bladed H-rotors on a shared vertical shaft, offset the inner rotor `60 deg` from the outer rotor, and spaced each three-blade rotor `120 deg` apart. The source also provided rotor dimensions, heights, chords, and pitch angles for full-scale and scaled configurations. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md)

We used that detail to write a more constrained prompt. We explicitly prohibited adding a Savonius rotor, helicity, taper, variable pitch, shrouds, and guide vanes. At the same time, we allowed the tool to infer support hardware such as hubs, struts, bearings, and a generator mount, but we kept those choices as editable assumptions. We drew that boundary because the paper supported the aerodynamic rotor layout but did not provide a complete fabrication design for the hidden mechanical details. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md)

We saw the first ZooKeeper result pass several tool-level checks, including constrained sketches and successful execution, while still leaving us with disconnected blades. We added a rotating assembly, stationary assembly, adjustable rotor azimuth, bearing concepts, hub bores, struts, and blade brackets in follow-up revisions. We did not eliminate the visual defects simply by making the code run. We recorded floating blocks around the inner blades, a missing inner top strut after one repair, and incorrect inner brackets after another. Anna also found that changing the `rotorAzimuth` parameter could disrupt the inner geometry and freeze the tool. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md; source: active/_anna daily log.md)

We learned the clearest VJ20 lesson from that failure: a successful execution report is not an engineering verification. It only tells us that the program could produce geometry, not that we had connected, mechanically coherent, source-faithful, CFD-ready geometry. By reading the KCL files and debugging one issue manually, Anna built a working mental model of how the rotor worked, but she also documented that we still needed CAD expertise to know where to look when generated geometry failed. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md)

## Comparing ZooKeeper with Fusion AI

We also tried Fusion's AI tools using the VJ20 prompt. Fusion gave us a much faster and visually promising first result, and Anna produced both full-scale and `1/11.01` scaled VJ20 models for a later simulation attempt. We saw Fusion as a strong option for rapid concept generation, especially when ZooKeeper suffered server, loading, or interaction problems. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md)

However, we found the same verification problem in a different form. We initially thought the Fusion model represented the dual-H-rotor concept well, but closer inspection showed us that its inner blades did not use the required DU 06-W-200 asymmetric airfoil. We treated that as important because the inner blade profile is not decorative; it supports the published design's aerodynamic claim. We could use Fusion for a fast visual starting point, but we could not call its output a faithful VJ20 reproduction until we checked the actual section geometry. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md)

We also learned that speed is not the only useful CAD metric. If we create a model quickly but cannot inspect, edit, or trace it back to source dimensions, we create downstream work. In one airfoil task, Fusion generated a profile facing the wrong direction despite explicit instructions, and the tool sometimes became slow or errored during use. We could manage those workflow problems, but they reinforced our need to inspect geometry before connecting it to CFD. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md)

## CAD became part of CFD validation, not a separate stage

We soon merged our CAD work with CFD learning. We used simple generated geometries and random airfoils to learn SimScale, then created and inspected NACA airfoil STEP geometry while troubleshooting an airfoil-validation case. When we redrew an airfoil in Fusion, we did not solve the coefficient discrepancy by geometry alone. That experience made us distinguish between a geometrically plausible model and a validated simulation input. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md; source: active/documentation/CFD log/Airfoil Validation Studies.md)

For VJ20, we separated a mechanically complete assembly from a CFD-clean model. Before we can use a CFD version, we need to define unambiguous rotating and stationary groups, correctly select blade and shaft surfaces, create a fluid region and rotating zone, and remove accidental gaps, overlaps, or tiny irrelevant faces. We documented those as future modeling requirements, not as proof that the current model already met them. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md)

## What we would do differently

If we repeated the process, we would start every model with a more formal geometry ledger: source-backed values in one column, inferred dimensions in another, and unknowns that block a faithful reconstruction in a third. We would inspect every generated revision against a checklist: blade count, airfoil identity and orientation, attachment continuity, strut count, rotor phase, overlaps, and whether we can select the required faces for CFD. We offer this as a recommendation based on our recorded failures, not as a tested universal CAD workflow. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/b46becaa-ac7e-4343-91b5-307ebca919dc.md)

We would also preserve the useful division of labor that emerged: we would use AI to generate a constrained first pass, use source records to challenge every important feature, and learn enough of the underlying model structure to make focused human edits. We did not show that AI can replace CAD expertise. We showed that AI can make early exploration faster while making traceability, geometry inspection, and intentional learning more important than before. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md)

## Where the models stand

We still use the VA9 model as a useful parameterized approximation of the published concept, but we need authoritative profile coordinates and a successful robust union before we treat it as a final solid. We use the Fusion VJ20 model as a useful visual and CFD-planning starting point, but we need to verify its inner airfoil and other geometry independently. We cannot use either model alone to establish manufacturability, structural adequacy, aerodynamic performance, or CFD validity. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md)

We do not treat that result as a failure. We moved from papers to geometry while learning new tools, and we produced more than a set of turbine files. We produced a documented method for future teams: preserve the evidence, label assumptions, make the model editable, inspect every revision, and do not confuse a polished rendering with an engineering conclusion. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md)

# Short Version: What AI-CAD Taught Us About Modeling a VAWT

We used AI-assisted CAD to turn two VAWT research designs, VA9 and VJ20, into editable turbine models. The tools let us generate complicated first-pass assemblies quickly, so we could compare the two concepts and begin preparing for CFD. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md)

We did not treat the fast models as automatically reliable models. We lacked complete published airfoil coordinates and hidden structural dimensions for VA9, so we labeled its blade geometry as an approximation. We had stronger published rotor specifications for VJ20, but ZooKeeper produced disconnected and floating parts during edits, while Fusion created an inner blade that did not match the required asymmetric airfoil. (source: active/documentation/CAD log/j710_va9_initial.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/a_vj20.md)

We learned that AI-CAD is useful for rapid exploration, not a substitute for source checking or engineering review. We saw that a model can execute successfully, look polished, and still contain disconnected geometry, incorrect airfoils, or unresolved assumptions. We now preserve the source-backed dimensions, label every inference, inspect each revision visually, and validate the final geometry before we treat it as ready for manufacturing or CFD. (source: active/_anna daily log.md; source: active/_Julie_daily_log.md; source: active/documentation/CAD log/CAD Modeling Process.md)
